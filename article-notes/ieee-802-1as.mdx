## What it solves

[IEEE 802.1AS](https://1.ieee802.org/tsn/802-1as-2020/) defines the **generalized Precision Time Protocol (gPTP)**, a Layer 2 profile of [IEEE 1588](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/1588/6825/) PTP. It solves three problems (LS19 §IV-A):

1. building a **synchronisation hierarchy** in the network;
2. **distributing time** from the root of that hierarchy to every device;
3. **measuring link delays** between neighbours at run time.

Synchronised clocks serve three purposes: coordinating transmissions ([Qbv](./ieee-802-1qbv.mdx)), timestamping critical events, and coordinating tasks such as reading all sensors at the same instant. All bridges and end stations must be **time-aware** (YSH19 §4.2).

## The synchronisation spanning tree

A **best master clock algorithm** elects one **grandmaster** among the grandmaster-capable devices. Each port then takes a state: **master**, **slave**, **passive** (breaks loops) or **disabled**. The result is a spanning tree rooted at the grandmaster.

## Sync, Follow_Up and the correction field

1. The grandmaster sends **Sync** on its master ports. Each device timestamps the reception, sends a new Sync on its own master ports, and timestamps the transmission. The difference is the **residence time** in that device.
2. Resetting the clock on each Sync would already synchronise the devices, but badly: the time horizon is limited to one Sync period, and devices far from the grandmaster reset later.
3. **Follow_Up** fixes both. It carries the **preciseOriginTimestamp** (when the grandmaster sent Sync, on a long time scale such as UTC), and a **correctionField** to which each device adds its residence time.
4. Two refinements: a peer-delay protocol **measures link delay** continuously and adds it to the correctionField; the **rate ratio** of neighbouring clocks is measured, chained to obtain the rate relative to the grandmaster, and used to scale residence times.

## 802.1AS-2011 versus 802.1AS-Rev

| | 802.1AS-2011 | 802.1AS-Rev (published as 802.1AS-2020) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Target | Audio/video (one of the three AVB standards) | Professional A/V and time-sensitive control |
| Accuracy | Better than 1 µs in implementations | Same, plus per-node error budgets |
| Grandmaster loss | A new one is elected, but the handover is not instantaneous and can cause time jumps | **Several grandmasters and several synchronisation trees**, one per **gPTP domain**, for seamless handover |
| Tree | Built by the best master algorithm | Can also be **configured explicitly** |

Several domains are separate instances of gPTP; link-delay measurement can be shared between them to save cost.

## Accuracy figures

- **Simulation** (Gutierrez et al., cited in LS19): at most **1 µs** difference between clocks up to 30 hops, **2 µs** up to 100 hops, without failures.
- **Testbed** (XZZ23 §IV): on eight TTTech bridges with the Linux PTP stack, the measured synchronisation error, together with propagation delay (2–6 ns on a single direct cable) and processing delay (bounded by 1.9 µs per hop), is what TAS schedules must budget for. XZZ23 also notes that 802.1AS-Rev allows a **per-node** synchronisation error, depending on role and hop distance to the grandmaster, instead of one global value.

## Open issues raised by LS19

- **Security.** Many attacks are handled by cryptography, but a **delay attack** is not: a compromised bridge lies about residence time in the correctionField, and every device below it drifts without noticing. Intrusion detection is proposed as a mitigation.
- **Failure model.** Some industries (aerospace) assume **Byzantine** failures of complex chips. 802.1AS provides redundant grandmasters and trees but does not say how to build a network that tolerates a given failure assumption, nor how to **merge** time from redundant grandmasters.
- **Start-up and restart.** Neither initial synchronisation nor a system-wide restart is specified, so there is no bound on the time to become synchronised after certain failures. SAE AS6802 could run on top.
- **Asymmetric failures** can leave a device unsynchronised without detection. It may then send at the wrong time, or open [Qbv](./ieee-802-1qbv.mdx) gates at the wrong instants, and frames can be lost even with [FRER](./ieee-802-1cb.mdx).

## In DetNet

DetNet relies on the same mechanisms (IEEE 1588 and 802.1AS) when it needs a common time reference (YSH19 §5.1). RFC 8655 notes that some DetNet queue-control techniques need sub-microsecond synchronisation.
