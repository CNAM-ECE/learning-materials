## What it does

[IEEE 802.1CB](https://1.ieee802.org/tsn/802-1cb/) lowers the probability of losing a packet by:

1. giving each packet of a stream a **sequence number**;
2. **replicating** it, in the source or in a relay;
3. sending the copies over **separate paths**;
4. **eliminating** duplicates at or near the destination.

It does **not** create the paths. They come from 802.1Q, from [802.1Qca](/blog/ieee-802-1qca-path-control) or from a CNC configured with [802.1Qcc](./ieee-802-1qcc.mdx).

## Stream identification

A stream is a sequence of packets from one talker to one or more listeners. The **stream_handle** is a local integer, with no required field in the frame. Identification is **passive** (it only inspects frames) or **active** (it rewrites addresses so that bridges can recognise the stream).

| Function | Type | Inspects | Modifies | Suited to |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Null stream | Passive | Destination MAC, VLAN | — | One (destination MAC, VLAN) pair per stream, as in AVB |
| Source MAC and VLAN | Passive | Source MAC, VLAN | — | All traffic from one (source MAC, VLAN) pair is one stream |
| Active destination MAC and VLAN | Active | Destination MAC, VLAN | Destination MAC, VLAN, priority | Talkers or proxies that translate a stream to a recognisable address, and listeners that restore it |
| IP stream | Passive | MAC, VLAN, IP addresses, DSCP, protocol, ports | — | Unicast IP streams, often combined with the active function |

## Elimination and ordering

- The eliminator keeps a **window of acceptable sequence numbers**; it delivers the first copy of each number and drops later ones.
- With a window larger than one, frames can be delivered **out of order**. This is the cheap choice; in-order delivery would need buffering until earlier numbers arrive.
- An **individual recovery** function removes repeats from a **stuck transmitter** that sends the same packet again and again.
- 16-bit sequence numbers and per-source numbering give some interoperability with HSR and PRP (IEC 62439-3) and with RFC 3985 pseudowires.
- The elimination logic has been checked by model checking.

## Reliability and availability

- **Reliability**: probability of correct operation from start-up to time $t$ (with CB: at least one copy delivered).
- **Availability**: $\text{MTTF} / (\text{MTTF} + \text{MTTR})$, which accounts for repair.
- Better network reliability raises MTTF, and with it the availability of the whole system.

Two conditions for this to hold:

1. **No single point of failure.** At least two disjoint paths, **including** the talker's and listeners' own links: an end station needs two physical links.
2. **Composition with end-to-end (E2E) safety protocols.**

## Why FRER and E2E protection do not compose

E2E protocols (checksums, sequence numbers, timestamps in the application) treat the network as a **black channel**. Their safety case requires the network to know nothing about the E2E checks. So the eliminator cannot tell a corrupted copy from a good one:

- CB therefore does **not improve integrity**: the first copy wins, even if it is wrong.
- For highly critical automotive messages, LS19 suggests doing redundancy **in the application**, and keeping CB for medium-criticality traffic.
- CB still helps the many industrial systems that need reliability but not E2E safety protocols.

## Path control: IEEE 802.1Qca

802.1Qca extends **IS-IS** shortest path bridging with **explicit trees**, so selected traffic can take a path other than the shortest one, together with bandwidth reservation and protection (redundant trees, loop-free alternates, restoration after a failure). LS19 leaves it aside because industrial and automotive networks are usually **engineered**: their topology is fixed at design time.
