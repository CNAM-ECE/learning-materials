## Switched Ethernet in three tasks

A switched Ethernet network has **end stations**, which produce and consume data, and **bridges** (the articles use "switch" as a synonym), which relay frames between them. LS19 sorts the forwarding work of a bridge into three tasks, and TSN extends two of them:

| Task | Question it answers | Where in 802.1Q clause 8.6 | TSN standards |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Traffic switching** | To which transmission ports does this frame go? | Frame filtering (8.6.3) | — |
| **Traffic shaping** | Which waiting frame is sent next on a port? | Queuing frames, queue management, transmission selection (8.6.6–8.6.8) | Qbv, Qch, Qbu, Qcr |
| **Traffic policing** | Does this frame or stream behave as agreed? | Ingress and egress filtering, flow metering (8.6.2, 8.6.4, 8.6.5) | Qci |

A bridge that guarantees **line-speed switching** forwards any incoming pattern without loss as long as no output port is overloaded. The hard case is minimum-size frames arriving back to back on every port: the switching decision must finish well within the reception time of one minimum frame, which needs a hardware switching fabric at high link speeds.

## The frame and its VLAN tag

<SourceFigure
  id="lobello2019-frame"
  caption="Figure 2. An Ethernet frame with the 4-byte 802.1Q VLAN tag. The tag carries the TPID (0x8100), the 3-bit priority code point, the drop eligible indicator and the 12-bit VLAN identifier."
/>

- The **priority code point (PCP)** gives one of eight priorities. Before AVB and TSN, a bridge mapped PCP to one of up to eight **traffic class queues** per port (802.1Q Table 8-5, rationale in Annex I) and served them by **strict priority**.
- Queues are FIFO: a frame never overtakes another inside one queue.
- A common TSN choice identifies a **stream** by its destination MAC address and VLAN ID. A frame is one instance on one link; a stream groups all the frames of one talker-to-listener distribution (LS19 §II-B).

## Store-and-forward or cut-through

802.1Q is vague on **cut-through**, where a bridge starts sending a frame before it has received all of it. LS19 assumes **store-and-forward**. Cut-through matters for motion control and daisy-chained industrial networks. The arguments against it are that a frame with a bad checksum may be forwarded and that configuration becomes more complex. (The project P802.1DU on cut-through bridges was later opened; see the [IEEE 802.1 post](/blog/ieee-802-1-tsn-task-group).)

## The 802.1Q formalism

- 802.1Q, *Bridges and Bridged Networks*, is the central document. TSN projects amend it, and it was close to 2,000 pages in 2018.
- Clause 5 is the **conformance clause**: what a device must, should or may implement to be called a bridge. TSN is about the **VLAN bridge** of clause 5.4.
- Naming: a capital letter after 802.1 (802.1**CB**, 802.1**CM**) is a standalone standard; lower-case letters (802.1Q**bu**) are an amendment to 802.1Q (YSH19 §4.2).

## The TSN toolbox

TSN is a **toolbox**: a designer picks only the standards an application needs. LS19 groups them under four pillars, and notes that the groups overlap (802.1AS-Rev serves both timing and reliability).

## Assessment of TSN as a whole (LS19 §VI)

| Category | Verdict in LS19 |
| --- | --- |
| Bandwidth | Follows Ethernet: 10 Mbit/s to 100 Gbit/s; TSN is independent of the PHY |
| Real-time | TSN's strongest point (Qav, Qbu/3br, Qbv, Qcr). Not yet at its minimum latency because store-and-forward is assumed |
| Jitter | With Qbv or Qch, jitter can shrink to the precision of clock synchronisation |
| Reliability | CB against loss, Qci against babbling devices; weak against asymmetric clock failures |
| Integrity | Only the Ethernet CRC; CB does not improve it |
| Confidentiality | Not standardised by TSN; left to the 802.1 security task group |
| Flexibility | Online reconfiguration through Qcc, but no worst-case bound on the time to compute a new configuration |
| Scalability | Large address space; synchronisation stays under 2 µs at 100 hops; Qbv scheduling is NP-complete |

LS19 adds **configuration ease**, which it rates poorly: the configuration space is huge, and profiles (such as 802.1BA for AVB) are the main help, with more guidance expected as adoption grows. Its informal star ratings (Table III) put bandwidth, scalability, real-time and flexibility as **strong**, reliability and availability as **medium**, and integrity, confidentiality and configuration as **weak**. Hence TSN as it stands is **not safety-critical** on its own.

**Research challenges** listed at the end of LS19:

1. **RC1. Configuration synthesis**: which mechanisms to use, and schedules that scale.
2. **RC2. Dependability** and design assurance.
3. **RC3. Security**, since IT/OT convergence opens cyber-physical systems to attacks.
4. **RC4. Verification and validation** by independent parties.

GPB22 surveys the work on RC1; see [Network design and synthesis](./tsn-network-design.mdx).

The flexibility row is the gap the doctoral project addresses: a reconfiguration that must finish within a deadline cannot yet be served by TSN with a guarantee (see [Dynamic reconfiguration](/blog/dynamic-reconfiguration-of-tsn)).
