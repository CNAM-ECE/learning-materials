## The mechanism

[IEEE 802.1Qbv](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.1Qbv/6068/) brings **time-triggered** communication into 802.1. Its particular choice is to schedule **queues, not frames** (LS19 §IV-B):

- Each queue has a **gate**, open or closed. Transmission selection only considers queues whose gate is open.
- A **gate control list (GCL)** says when each gate opens and closes. It repeats with a **cycle time** and has a limited number of entries (typically 8 to 1024, depending on the bridge, XZZ23 §II-A).
- The standard actually stores relative durations between gate changes; LS19 writes them as absolute times for clarity.
- End stations must implement the same gates, but at their interface, frame-level scheduling cannot be told apart from gate scheduling.
- The network-wide schedule is computed by a **CNC** (XZZ23 §I).

**Guard band.** A queue must stop starting new frames early enough that the last one finishes before its gate closes; otherwise it would delay the queue opening at that instant. This interval is the **guard band**. A simple bridge reserves one maximum frame time; a better one checks whether the next frame still fits. Windows protected this way are called **protected windows**.

Priorities alone cannot keep ES3 from delaying ES1, because a low-priority frame already on the wire cannot be stopped. A GCL entry that closes queue 4 (ES3) in time, while queue 7 (ES1) is open, solves it.

## Computing the GCL

The gate mechanism is simple; finding the gate times is not. Schedule synthesis is in general **NP-complete** (LS19). Two families of methods exist: dedicated search (heuristics, meta-heuristics, genetic algorithms) and general solvers (ILP, SMT). XZZ23 reviews them with three groups of models:

| Model group | Choices | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Network** | Propagation and processing delay, line rate, queues for TT traffic (≤ 8), maximum GCL length, synchronisation error | Constraints that a schedule must respect on real hardware |
| **Traffic** | Fully or partially schedulable release times; strictly or non-strictly periodic; payload over several frames; implicit, constrained or arbitrary deadline; zero-jitter or bounded jitter | What the application allows the scheduler to choose |
| **Scheduling** | **No-wait** (no queuing along the path) or **wait-allowed**; frame or stream isolation in queues; joint routing; fragmentation; preemption | The search space and the delay and jitter obtained |

## What the 17-method comparison found

XZZ23 ran 17 TAS scheduling methods (2016–2022) in simulation and on an eight-bridge testbed:

<SourceFigure
  id="xue2023-fig6"
  caption="Figure 3. The testbed: eight TTTech evaluation boards in a ring, Raspberry Pi end stations with Intel i210 NICs, and a CNC and CUC."
/>

- **No method wins everywhere.** Each model or method is best only under some settings.
- **Settings bias conclusions.** Results depend heavily on the stream sets, topologies and resource limits chosen. Use broad settings, or realistic ones taken from profiles such as [IEC/IEEE 60802](./tsn-profiles.mdx).
- **Metrics can disagree.** The schedulable ratio and a pairwise schedulability advantage gave inconsistent rankings; several metrics, with statistically sound data, are needed.
- **Describe the experiment fully**: model assumptions, solver settings, stream specifications, and exactly where delay is measured.
- **Model assumptions hold on hardware.** Propagation delay (a few ns), processing delay (≤ 1.9 µs per hop) and synchronisation error can be bounded on the testbed, which justifies the constant-or-bounded assumptions of most methods.
- **Constraints often ignored**: the maximum GCL length of real bridges, and the limited number of queues.

## The scheduling models in detail (from the margin notes)

| Choice | Options | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scheduling entity** | Frame (one GCL entry per frame, no interference); window (frames share an entry, causing jitter; window assigned, partially or not); class (asynchronous, out of scope) | Frame-based gives determinism; window-based gives short GCLs |
| **Queue isolation** | Unrestricted (as in TTEthernet; ignores FIFO order, so the schedule may deviate); explicit: FIFO order kept, **frame isolation** (one frame of a stream in the queue at a time, robust to loss) or **stream isolation** (stricter) | Only wait-allowed models are affected |
| **Routing** | **FR**: fixed routes, usually shortest paths; **JRS**: joint routing and scheduling | JRS schedules more but costs more |
| **Fragmentation versus preemption** | FRAG: schedule-aware fragmentation cuts store-and-forward delay but adds headers; PRE: express interrupts preemptable | PRE gives no delay gain over FRAG (reassembly) but saves bandwidth |
| **Non-overlap constraint** | Big-M (ILP), disjunction (SMT), AllDifferent (CP) | CP is faster than SMT and ILP |

A maximum Ethernet frame is 1,522 bytes, including a 22-byte header with the VLAN tag. Payloads above 1,500 bytes are split into frames, scheduled one by one or back to back as a block.

**The 17 methods.**

| Class | Methods |
| --- | --- |
| FR, no-wait | SMT-NW (job-shop ILP with guard-band compression and tabu), SMT-FRAG (SMT with fragments), DT (divisibility theory, incremental search without backtracking) |
| FR, wait-allowed | SMT-WA (first queue isolation in TSN), AT (array theory for windows), I-OMT (iterative OMT by groups), CP-WA (constraint programming with decomposition), SMT-PRE (SMT choosing express or preemptable), LS-TB (list scheduling with backtracking on conflict sets), LS-PL (per-link phases) |
| JRS | JRS-WA (first joint ILP, prunes infeasible paths), JRS-MC (multicast), JRS-NW and JRS-NW-L (ILP with indicator constraints, speed-ups), LS (list scheduling over routes, no backtracking), I-ILP (iterative ILP with grouping and multicast routing), CG (conflict-graph independent sets, heuristic plus ILP) |

Other objectives in the literature: minimum delay and jitter, minimum number of queues, better QoS for coexisting BE or AVB traffic, reliability (retransmission, disjoint routes, FRER, out-of-sync tolerance), **online reconfiguration** (adding and removing streams, two-stage updates that avoid deadlock), and learning-based schedulers (kNN, GCN with DRL), which give no determinism guarantee.

**Testbed measurements.**

| Quantity | Measured | Used in simulation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Propagation delay (RTT on a CAT7 cable) | 2–6 ns | 6 ns |
| Processing delay (inferred) | ≤ 1.9 µs | 1.9 µs |
| Synchronisation error, after grandmaster election (about 5 s) | ≤ 10 ns | 10 ns |
| System clock to PTP hardware clock offset | ≤ 50 ns | 50 ns |

16 of the 17 methods ran on the hardware (SMT-FRAG produced fragments too small for the hardware windows). On a validation set of 8 streams with 1 ms periods and 10,000 frames, the **measured delay never exceeded the analysed worst case**.

**Simulation set-up.** 10 to 220 streams (220 is above industrial utilisation bounds); periods from 50 µs to 4 ms in six types, so the cycle is their LCM; five payload types; line, ring, tree and mesh topologies of 8 to 78 bridges (the 802.1AS hop limit); 1 Gbit/s; 8 queues all for TT traffic. 38,400 instances, each with a 2 h and 4 GB limit; Python with Z3 and Gurobi. Two metrics: the **schedulable ratio** (unknown counted as unschedulable, so a lower bound) and the pairwise **schedulability advantage** on known results.

**Findings.**

1. **F1. Complex models help in theory, their overhead hurts in practice.** JRS beats FR on advantage but its ratio drops by 41.8 % against 10.9 % as the size grows, worse on rings and meshes. FRAG/PRE have the best advantage (~14 %) but schedule under 10 % beyond 70 streams. Wait-allowed beats no-wait on advantage; no-wait beats wait-allowed under load. Full schedulability beats partial: choosing release times at the end stations matters more than at the bridges. Frame-based beats window-based because GCL length limits windows.
2. **F2. No global best.** Exact methods win below about 100 streams, heuristics above, and heuristics exploit complex models better (CG over JRS-NW, I-OMT over I-ILP).
3. **F3. Workload hurts scalability more than network size.** Runtime and memory grow with the number of streams; more bridges give longer but sparser paths. This matters because the CNC is often embedded.
4. **F4. Schedule quality varies.** Frame-based GCLs can exceed 2,000 entries, while hardware supports at most 1,024; window-based and JRS schedules are shorter. Delay grows with bridges (92 to 192 µs); no-wait is lower and JRS higher. Jitter *falls* with load because windows get tighter. Unrestricted queue isolation uses too many queues.

**Constraints that real hardware adds.** PTP frames can collide with TT frames; an end station needs an inter-frame gap of about 50 µs; GCL windows have a granularity. The practical choice: simple or heuristic methods under heavy load, avoid JRS at scale, no-wait when few queues are available.

**Threats to validity** named by the authors: model, algorithm and implementation effects are not separated; only two window-based methods; some implementations are proofs of concept; parameters not tuned. Future work: avionics and automotive instances, fault tolerance, an open dataset.

## Scalability and jitter

- Scheduling scales poorly in theory, but recent solvers handle thousands of messages (LS19 §VI).
- With Qbv, jitter can be reduced to the precision of [clock synchronisation](./ieee-802-1as.mdx). A clock failure, though, makes the gates switch at the wrong times.
