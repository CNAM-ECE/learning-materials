GPB22 reviews how networks for **time-critical (TC)** distributed applications are designed: which strategies compute topology, routes, priorities, schedules and bandwidth, and what each one gains in reliability, timeliness and cost. The motivating systems are heterogeneous, mixed-criticality distributed systems: autonomous vehicles, smart factories and cities, sometimes with wireless parts. The configuration computations sit in the controller layer of an SDN architecture.

A buffer followed by a shaper is what decides **when** a frame leaves. **Contention** is several flows trying to use the same egress link at once.

## The lineage of deterministic Ethernet

| Step | What it added | Why it was not enough for TC traffic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shared-medium Ethernet** (bus) | Frame format, MAC addresses, collision detection | Collisions: no bound on delay |
| **Switched full-duplex Ethernet** | No collisions; a switch is a hub that reasons about the destination | Contention moves to the switch: an over-subscribed egress port drops frames, so delay and loss are unbounded |
| **Strict-priority Ethernet** (802.1D-1998, 802.1Q-2005) | Classification, priority queues, VLANs (a VLAN is a subset of ports forming a broadcast domain), the PCP mapping frames to queues | Priority alone gives no per-flow guarantee |
| **AFDX** (ARINC 664 part 7, 2005) | **Virtual links**: per-flow trees with a maximum frame length $L_{max}$ and a **bandwidth allocation gap** BAG (a power of two from 1 to 128 ms); FIFO queues | Jitter; one buffer per VL is costly; needs worst-case timing analysis; only eight BAG values; 100 Mbit/s |
| **TTEthernet** (SAE AS6802, 2011) | Fault-tolerant **clock synchronisation** and **time-triggered** traffic. Three classes: **TS** (time-scheduled) > **RC** (rate-constrained, the AFDX traffic) > **BE** | Per-flow queues are costly; schedules are hard to compute; needs synchronised clocks |
| **AVB** (802.1Qav, Qat, AS, merged in 802.1Q-2011) | Forwarding and queuing for time-sensitive streams: **per class** rather than per flow, with the credit-based shaper | Tens to hundreds of µs per path; no zero-jitter traffic |
| **TSN** (802.1Qbv and the other amendments) | TTEthernet's scheduled traffic **plus** AVB's per-class queues; mixed criticality; vendor-neutral | Schedules for Qbv are very hard to compute; needs synchronised clocks |

The shift from **event-triggered** to **time-triggered** communication: traffic is shaped in time by schedule tables computed in advance against a synchronised clock. Time-triggered designs are more predictable; event-triggered ones are cheaper to install and over-provision less. The survey's short summary: **TSN ≈ TTEthernet + AVB**, with the remaining limits being configuration complexity and clock synchronisation.

**CBS in this lineage.** The credit decreases at the send slope while a frame is sent, increases at the idle slope while frames wait, and is reset to zero when the queue empties. See [802.1Qav](./ieee-802-1qav.mdx).

**Integration modes.** Scheduled traffic can still be delayed by AVB or BE frames already on the wire. TSN offers two **integration modes**: a **guard band** (Qbv), and **preemption** (Qbu/802.3br), where the lower-priority frame is interrupted and sent as fragments afterwards. Combining both removes the residual delay of the smallest non-preemptable fragment and wastes less bandwidth than a full guard band.

## The five design problems

Computer-aided design of a TC network is split into tasks, each defined by **inputs, outputs, constraints and an optimisation objective**. The first two belong to the control plane, the last three to the management plane.

| Problem | Output | Validity constraints | Typical objectives |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Topology selection** (network planning) | Which switches and links, and how they connect, from a component library | Every source–destination pair connected; each switch has at least two links; fault-tolerant applications need disjoint paths | Minimum cost or weight |
| **2. Routing** | One route per flow: a path for unicast, a tree for multicast | All destinations reached, no cycle | Fewest links, minimum weight, minimum maximum link load |
| **3. Priority / traffic-type assignment** | Critical or non-critical class per flow; priorities | Fits the protocol (SP, AFDX, AVB) | TS > BS > non-critical; a type can span several priorities |
| **4. Scheduling** (TS traffic) | When each frame is sent on each link. In TSN: the **GCL**, the number of queues per port and the flow-to-queue mapping | Link occupancy, flow dependencies along the path (with synchronisation error), end-to-end deadline, disjoint TS queue intervals (relaxed when frames of the same flow share a queue) | Maximise laxity ($D - \text{WCD}$), minimise tardiness ($\text{WCD} - D$), minimise the number of TS queues |
| **5. Bandwidth allocation** (BS traffic) | An upper bound on the bandwidth of each non-scheduled flow, so that nothing is starved | $\sum$ bandwidth ≤ link bandwidth | Joint bandwidth and schedule optimisation in TTEthernet and TSN |

A configuration is **schedulable** when the worst-case delay (WCD) is at most the deadline. A fully deterministic schedule gives the WCD directly. Otherwise the WCD comes from an analysis: network calculus, or trajectory analysis for AFDX and TTEthernet.

:::caution Average versus worst case
Average delays of BS traffic are often computed with an M/M/1 queue ($\rho = \lambda/\mu$) or the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula. They are not guarantees. For TSN, extending the 802.1BA formula for CBS is **unsafe**; network calculus or availability-interval analysis is needed. The [CBS post](/blog/credit-based-shaper) explains the limits of that formula.
:::

## Strategies

<SourceFigure
  id="gavrilut2022-fig4"
  caption="Figure 3. Which strategies have been used for which design problems: k-shortest paths (KSP), ILP, constraint programming (CP), LP, GRASP, greedy heuristics (GH), tabu search (TST), genetic algorithms (GA), SMT and OMT."
/>

| Kind | Examples | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Constructive** (heuristics) | Greedy heuristics, list scheduling, KSP, ECMP | Fast; usable everywhere | No guarantee of finding a solution or of optimality |
| **Exhaustive / exact** | ILP, CP, SMT, OMT | Optimal when they finish | Exponential runtime, worse with more constraints |
| **Optimised** (metaheuristics) | Tabu search, GRASP, genetic algorithms | Trade speed against quality | Must be validated against exact methods on small cases |

A **quality function** ranks solutions; it is separate from validity. Outputs are compared on timeliness, throughput, optimality and runtime. "Complexity" in the survey is measured by **runtime**, not by algorithmic complexity.

## Results worth remembering

**Topology.** A labelling formulation with LP: maximal topology, then KSP, a scheduling heuristic and a greedy heuristic with adaptive arc weights. Duplicating the network is the costliest solution, the optimised one the cheapest, and the adaptive heuristic always found a feasible design, but its runtime grows roughly exponentially with the number of stations.

**Routing.**

- AVB routing over TSN with KSP and GRASP, judged on schedulability and worst-case delay: optimised routing made 4 of 5 realistic cases schedulable, against shortest path.
- Routing TS traffic with ILP to leave the most slack for non-scheduled traffic. Minimising only the maximum scheduled traffic load (MSTL), or MSTL plus hop count: the second halves the flow span and beats shortest path and ECMP by about 20 % in 22 of 24 cases, with 100 times the runtime.
- Fault-tolerant routing: minimise the maximum link bandwidth together with a replication level over disjoint routes. A greedy heuristic builds balanced routes, then tabu search temporarily removes congested links to reroute. It handled 1,000 flows in 25 s and 120 nodes in 520 s, with no transmission model or timing analysis.
- Load-adaptive routing lowers the maximum load by 18 % against shortest path and runs in time linear in the number of flows, but exponential in the number of nodes, so not at runtime.

**Priority assignment.** A genetic algorithm chooses priorities and the number of express queues for preemption (an individual is a priority vector plus an express-queue matrix; stopped after 100,000 generations). It scheduled 100 % of 400 synthetic tree topologies with better reliability than the FPCP, AOPA and RPA baselines, but its runtime limits it to offline use. For ATS, priorities are chosen to minimise the number of queues.

**Bandwidth.** In AVB, bandwidth allocation means tuning the idle slopes, for example to minimise average and worst-case delay.

**Scheduling.**

- Flow versus frame isolation solved with SMT/OMT on 4–12 nodes: runtime from 1 s to 4 h; flow isolation schedules 13 % fewer cases. The choice matters only in loaded networks; frame isolation needs at most two TS queues.
- **No-wait** scheduling to minimise guard bands (wasted bandwidth): tabu search on flow order plus a heuristic for start times, with five initial strategies and back-to-back compression. Against a 5 h ILP: 3 % shorter flow span, equal or better in 21 cases, polynomial runtime (1,500 flows in about 3 h). Compression removes 24 % of gate events when it has an effect. Follow-ups: a faster ILP, adding flows at runtime, GRASP.
- With a limited number of **gate entries**, schedules use time windows (array theory). Runtime is driven by the number of windows (more than two takes hours); more windows give less jitter. Window-based schedules beat frame-based ones only with one or two windows.

**Scale.** List scheduling handled 402 nodes in 60 s, and ECMP is probably fast enough for online use (not shown). SMT is not: more than 300 s for frame isolation and more than 15 s for flow isolation.

## Open problems

- **Where to compute.** AVB was distributed; TSN moves to centralised configuration (802.1Qcc). The best place for the computing architecture depends on the domain: on board for vehicles and aircraft, in the cloud for smart cities and factories. Greedy heuristics run anywhere; optimisation belongs on a server, possibly in parallel over independent subsets of flows.
- **Online design.** Two questions: (1) how to find a configuration into which new flows can be integrated, which points to **porous schedules** that leave room; (2) how dynamic time-aware networks really are (how fast and how often flows arrive), still open, which points to **elastic scheduling**.
- **Optimal configuration** with the best benefit at the lowest cost using off-the-shelf hardware, across the five tasks and the criteria of reliability, timeliness, throughput, cost and runtime.

TSN is a standard suite: it lowers cost against proprietary networks (low NRE cost) in avionics, automotive, industrial automation and fronthaul, but only with proper design. The online-design questions are the ones the doctoral project takes up; see [Dynamic reconfiguration](/blog/dynamic-reconfiguration-of-tsn).
