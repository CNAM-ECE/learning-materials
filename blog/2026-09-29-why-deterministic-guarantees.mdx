Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) promises that a standard Ethernet network can carry control traffic with *bounded* latency. This first post explains what "bounded" means, why it cannot be established by measurement alone, and why deterministic network calculus is the analytical tool of choice for establishing it.

## 1. The promise of TSN

TSN is a family of IEEE 802.1 amendments that add deterministic behaviour to bridged Ethernet. Industrial guides typically single out four of them [3]:

| Standard | Mechanism | Role in the guarantee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 802.1AS | Generalised Precision Time Protocol (gPTP) | Common time base for all bridges and end stations |
| 802.1Qbv | Time-Aware Shaper (TAS) | Gate control lists open and close queues at scheduled instants |
| 802.1Qci | Per-Stream Filtering and Policing (PSFP) | Enforces the traffic contract declared by each stream |
| 802.1CB | Frame Replication and Elimination (FRER) | Seamless redundancy over disjoint paths |

A shorter glossary is kept in the [TSN glossary](/reference/tsn-glossary).

The promise is converged traffic: control loops, video and best-effort traffic share one physical network, and the control traffic still meets its deadlines. That promise is only as strong as the argument that supports it.

## 2. Average-case versus worst-case performance

Classical queueing theory answers questions such as "what is the *mean* delay of a packet?" or "what is the probability that the delay exceeds 1 ms?". Such answers are indispensable for dimensioning best-effort networks, but they are the wrong kind of answer for a safety-critical control loop, for two reasons.

1. **Rare events are exactly the events that matter.** A braking command delayed once in $10^9$ frames is acceptable in a statistical sense and unacceptable in an engineering sense.
2. **Stochastic assumptions are hard to justify.** Poisson arrivals and independence between flows are modelling conveniences; certification authorities ask for arguments that hold for *every* admissible traffic pattern.

Deterministic network calculus replaces probabilistic assumptions with *envelopes*: each flow is assumed only to respect an upper bound on the amount of data it can emit in any interval, and each network element is assumed only to guarantee a lower bound on the service it provides. From these two envelopes it derives bounds that hold for every trajectory compatible with them.

## 3. Why measurement does not suffice

The practical TSN migration guide by TSF Technology proposes a five-phase method: audit the existing network and identify critical flows, design the TSN topology and synchronisation domains, pilot on a segment, configure scheduled traffic with 802.1Qbv, and finally *validate latency and jitter against requirements* [3]. The last phase is usually carried out by measurement.

Measurement establishes a *lower* bound on the worst-case delay: the largest observed value. A certificate needs an *upper* bound. The gap between the two can be large, because the worst case arises from a precise alignment of bursts on every hop of a path, which a test campaign is unlikely to provoke. In the companion repository on certified admission control, packet-level simulation of 4,915 admitted flows observed a median worst delay of only 19 % (control traffic) and 10 % (stream traffic) of the analytical bound [4]. These figures are a sanity check, not a tightness measure: they illustrate that the observed maximum and the provable maximum are different quantities.

:::note[Key distinction]
A measured maximum answers "what happened?". A network calculus bound answers "what *can* happen, under the stated assumptions?". Only the second can be used as a guarantee.
:::

## 4. Static configuration and the reconfiguration problem

TSN guarantees are usually established for a configuration that is computed and verified offline, then frozen. In practice, networks change: flows are added and removed, links fail, and production lines are reconfigured. Two recent lines of work, discussed in [post 9](/blog/dynamic-reconfiguration-of-tsn), illustrate the tension:

- Sertbaş Bülbül, Ergenç and Fischer use a software-defined networking (SDN) controller to migrate time-sensitive flows to new paths at run time [5];
- Boyer and Henia design a Time-Aware Shaper schedule that can absorb rescue flows after a permanent link fault without modifying existing windows [6].

Both raise the same question: *after* the change, does every admitted flow still meet its deadline? Answering it quickly and soundly is the motivation for this series.
