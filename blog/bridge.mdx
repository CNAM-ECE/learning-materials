## 1. Definition

A **bridge** relays [Ethernet](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.3/10422/) frames between two or more LANs at Layer 2. The bridges of TSN are specified in [IEEE 802.1Q](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.1Q/10323/), *Bridges and Bridged Networks*, which also defines [virtual LANs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VLAN) [1][4].

Four principles from the IEEE 802.1 tutorial frame everything else [1]:

- **MAC addresses identify, they do not locate.** A [MAC address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAC_address) names a station, not where it is. The tutorial calls this a major Layer 2 value, not a defect.
- **Forwarding uses the destination MAC address and the VLAN ID.**
- **Unknown destinations are flooded, known ones are filtered.** A frame whose destination location is not known is sent to every port of its VLAN except the one it arrived on. Once the location is known, the other ports are filtered out.
- **Quality of service comes after the forwarding decision.** It is based on the frame's priority, its drop eligibility, and time.

The last principle is where TSN enters: almost every TSN tool acts after the bridge has decided where a frame goes, and decides *when* and *whether* it goes there.

## 2. Architecture: control plane and data plane

Figure 1 separates the two planes [2][3]:

- the **control plane** runs the control protocols as *higher layer entities* inside the bridge. Alternatively, an **external agent**, such as an [SDN controller](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7426), provides the control instead of the distributed protocols;
- the **data plane** is made of a **MAC relay** and **at least two ports**.

The external agent is what the [SDN post](/blog/sdn-and-tsn-configuration) calls the CNC. The standard's own, more detailed picture is Figure 8-2, "VLAN-aware Bridge architecture", of 802.1Q [2].

<SourceFigure
  id="ieee8021q-tutorial-bridge-components"
  caption="Figure 2. Specification versus implementation. 802.1Q describes a bridge as components (here A and B) whose ports have distinct types according to their role. A real bridge often implements all of them on a single relay, with one port combining several functions."
/>

The standard describes a bridge as a set of **components**, one per type of Layer 2 virtual network, each with typed ports. This is a description language: it specifies the operation in distinct steps. It does not prescribe an implementation. Implementations often use a single MAC relay, and one physical port may implement several port functions. When the outermost EtherType of a frame does not match the component type, the reception port assigns the frame to a VLAN [1].

## 3. Data-plane actions

<SourceFigure
  id="farkas2018-data-plane-actions"
  caption="Figure 3. The data plane redrawn as a pipeline. Each stage applies an action set, using its own table. “Baggy pants” is just the simple drawing of the same pipeline."
/>

The baggy-pants drawing can be unfolded into a pipeline of three stages. Each applies an **action set** driven by a table [2][3]:

| Stage | Action set | Actions | TSN additions at this stage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ingress port | Action set 1 | Filtering (drop), tagging and untagging, VID translation, de- and encapsulation | Stream identification, [PSFP](/blog/per-stream-filtering-and-policing), the ingress side of [FRER](/blog/frame-replication-and-elimination) |
| Relay | Action set 2 | Forwarding, filtering | Explicit trees ([802.1Qca](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.1Qca/5557/)), replication and elimination |
| Egress port | Action set 3 | Filtering, tagging and untagging, VID translation, de- and encapsulation, metering, queuing, transmission selection | [CBS](/blog/credit-based-shaper), [ATS](/blog/asynchronous-traffic-shaping), [TAS](/blog/time-aware-shaper), [CQF](/blog/cyclic-queuing-and-forwarding), [preemption](/blog/ieee-802-1qbu-frame-preemption) |

Most TSN tools therefore act on the egress port, where queuing and transmission selection happen.

## 4. The forwarding process

<SourceFigure
  id="farkas2018-forwarding-process"
  caption="Figure 4. The forwarding process of IEEE 802.1Q, from the reception port to the transmission port. Each box is a sub-clause of clause 8.6 of the standard. Frame filtering consults the filtering database, and queue management acts on the queues."
/>

Inside the data plane, 802.1Q specifies the forwarding process as a fixed sequence of steps, each in its own sub-clause of clause 8.6 [2][3]:

| Step | Sub-clause | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Active topology enforcement | 8.6.1 | Discards frames that would use a port not in the active topology for that frame, for example a port blocked by the spanning tree |
| Ingress | 8.6.2 | Assigns a VLAN to frames that arrive without one, and drops frames from ports that are not members of the frame's VLAN |
| Frame filtering | 8.6.3 | Looks up the destination MAC address and VLAN ID in the **filtering database** to choose the output ports |
| Egress | 8.6.4 | Applies the output port's VLAN membership and tagging rules |
| Flow metering | 8.6.5 | Meters frames against a bandwidth profile; per-stream filtering and policing extends this step |
| Queuing frames | 8.6.6 | Places each frame in the queue of its traffic class at the output port |
| Queue management | 8.6.7 | Removes frames from the queues, for example frames held too long |
| Transmission selection | 8.6.8 | Chooses the next frame to send, among the queues allowed to transmit |

Reading the TSN standards becomes much easier with this list in mind. Each amendment says which of these sub-clauses it changes. For example, [scheduled traffic](/blog/time-aware-shaper) adds gates to transmission selection (8.6.8), and [asynchronous traffic shaping](/blog/asynchronous-traffic-shaping) adds a shaper in front of it.

## 5. Quality-of-service functions

<SourceFigure
  id="farkas2018-qos-functions"
  caption="Figure 5. The QoS functions of a port, in order from frame reception to frame transmission. Filtering, policing and shaping can work per stream or per class; queuing and transmission selection work per class. Together they form a hierarchy."
/>

Figure 5 isolates the quality-of-service functions of a port [2]:

1. **Filtering and policing**, which can be per stream or per class;
2. **Shaping**, also per stream or per class;
3. **Queuing** and **transmission selection**, which are per class.

The order matters for analysis. Policing makes a stream respect its [arrival curve](/blog/arrival-curves) before it meets other traffic. Shaping changes the curve. Queuing and transmission selection then define the [service curve](/blog/service-curves) that each class receives.

## 6. What the control plane controls

<SourceFigure
  id="ieee8021q-tutorial-topology-layers"
  caption="Figure 6. The topology layers of a bridged network. Each layer is a subset, real or not, of the one below it: the physical network topology, the active topology, the VLAN topology, and the station locations (the MAC address topology)."
/>

The control plane maintains four layered views of the network [1][3]:

| Layer | Content | Maintained by, for example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical network topology | All links and bridges | Management (enabling and disabling ports) |
| Active topology | The links actually used: a spanning tree, shortest paths, or traffic-engineered paths | [RSTP](/blog/rstp-rapid-spanning-tree), [MSTP](/blog/mstp-multiple-spanning-tree), [SPB](/blog/ieee-802-1aq-shortest-path-bridging), [path control (PCR)](/blog/ieee-802-1qca-path-control) |
| VLAN topology | Which bridges and ports belong to each VLAN | [MVRP](/blog/mrp-and-mvrp), SPB, PCR, [SRP](/blog/ieee-802-1qat-srp) |
| Station location | Where each MAC address is | Source address learning, [MMRP](/blog/mrp-and-mvrp), SPB, PCR, SRP |

<SourceFigure
  id="intro2017-control-plane"
  caption="Figure 7. Control options. Each VLAN is assigned a control mode: spanning tree, shortest path bridging (IS-IS), or an external agent for software-defined VLANs. Several modes can coexist in one network, and an external agent can use non-802.1 protocols."
/>

Each of these protocols has its own post: [RSTP](/blog/rstp-rapid-spanning-tree), [MSTP](/blog/mstp-multiple-spanning-tree), [SPB](/blog/ieee-802-1aq-shortest-path-bridging), [path control](/blog/ieee-802-1qca-path-control), [MRP and MVRP](/blog/mrp-and-mvrp), and [SRP](/blog/ieee-802-1qat-srp).

Control modes are assigned per VLAN. Distributed protocols and an external agent can therefore coexist in the same network, each controlling its own VLANs. The external control can even be a non-802.1 protocol, such as PCE or GMPLS [3]. This hybrid model is what lets a TSN network keep spanning tree for ordinary traffic while a CNC installs explicit paths for streams.

## 8. From the articles

Reading notes on Lo Bello and Steiner [5] and Yang et al. [6], from the copies kept in the repository's [`ressource/`](https://github.com/CNAM-ECE/learning-materials/tree/main/ressource) folder. They expand the margin notes taken while reading.

### Switched Ethernet in three tasks

A switched Ethernet network has **end stations**, which produce and consume data, and **bridges** (the articles use "switch" as a synonym), which relay frames between them. Lo Bello and Steiner [5] sorts the forwarding work of a bridge into three tasks, and TSN extends two of them:

| Task | Question it answers | Where in 802.1Q clause 8.6 | TSN standards |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Traffic switching** | To which transmission ports does this frame go? | Frame filtering (8.6.3) | — |
| **Traffic shaping** | Which waiting frame is sent next on a port? | Queuing frames, queue management, transmission selection (8.6.6–8.6.8) | Qbv, Qch, Qbu, Qcr |
| **Traffic policing** | Does this frame or stream behave as agreed? | Ingress and egress filtering, flow metering (8.6.2, 8.6.4, 8.6.5) | Qci |

<SourceFigure
  id="lobello2019-forwarding"
  caption="Figure 9. The forwarding steps of 802.1Q clause 8.6, from reception port to transmission port, with the three tasks and the TSN amendments that act on each step."
/>

A bridge that guarantees **line-speed switching** forwards any incoming pattern without loss as long as no output port is overloaded. The hard case is minimum-size frames arriving back to back on every port: the switching decision must finish well within the reception time of one minimum frame, which needs a hardware switching fabric at high link speeds.

### The frame and its VLAN tag

<SourceFigure
  id="lobello2019-frame"
  caption="Figure 10. An Ethernet frame with the 4-byte 802.1Q VLAN tag. The tag carries the TPID (0x8100), the 3-bit priority code point, the drop eligible indicator and the 12-bit VLAN identifier."
/>

- The **priority code point (PCP)** gives one of eight priorities. Before AVB and TSN, a bridge mapped PCP to one of up to eight **traffic class queues** per port (802.1Q Table 8-5, rationale in Annex I) and served them by **strict priority**.
- Queues are FIFO: a frame never overtakes another inside one queue.
- A common TSN choice identifies a **stream** by its destination MAC address and VLAN ID. A frame is one instance on one link; a stream groups all the frames of one talker-to-listener distribution ([5] §II-B).

### Store-and-forward or cut-through

802.1Q is vague on **cut-through**, where a bridge starts sending a frame before it has received all of it. Lo Bello and Steiner [5] assumes **store-and-forward**. Cut-through matters for motion control and daisy-chained industrial networks. The arguments against it are that a frame with a bad checksum may be forwarded and that configuration becomes more complex. (The project P802.1DU on cut-through bridges was later opened; see the [IEEE 802.1 post](/blog/ieee-802-1-tsn-task-group).)

### The 802.1Q formalism

- 802.1Q, *Bridges and Bridged Networks*, is the central document. TSN projects amend it, and it was close to 2,000 pages in 2018.
- Clause 5 is the **conformance clause**: what a device must, should or may implement to be called a bridge. TSN is about the **VLAN bridge** of clause 5.4.
- Naming: a capital letter after 802.1 (802.1**CB**, 802.1**CM**) is a standalone standard; lower-case letters (802.1Q**bu**) are an amendment to 802.1Q ([6] §4.2).
