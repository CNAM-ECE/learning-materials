The credit-based shaper (CBS) is the oldest [TSN](https://1.ieee802.org/tsn/) shaper. It comes from [Audio Video Bridging](https://www.ieee802.org/1/pages/avbridges.html) (AVB). It needs no clock, only a counter per queue, and it turns bursts into an even flow of frames.

## 1. Definition

The **credit-based shaper** is a transmission selection algorithm for one traffic-class queue of an egress port. It was introduced by [IEEE 802.1Qav](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.1Qav/4401/) and is now part of [IEEE 802.1Q](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.1Q/10323/). Each shaped queue keeps a **credit**, counted in bits. A frame of that queue may start transmission only while the credit is zero or positive [2][3].

The credit changes according to two slopes:

| Parameter | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `idleSlope` | Rate at which credit grows while frames are waiting. It equals the bandwidth reserved for the class. |
| `sendSlope` | Rate at which credit falls during a transmission: `idleSlope − portTransmitRate`, which is negative. |
| `hiCredit`, `loCredit` | The largest and smallest values the credit can reach. They follow from the interfering frame sizes and the slopes. |

## 2. The credit rules

<CommonsFigure
  id="cbs-credit"
  caption="Figure 2. Credit of the credit-based shaper. While interfering traffic is sent, the credit of the waiting AVB queue rises with idleSlope. Each AVB frame then lowers it with sendSlope. A frame is held while the credit is negative (red background) and released when it returns to zero."
/>

Figure 2 follows these rules:

1. **Frames waiting, not transmitting:** the credit increases at `idleSlope`. This happens when the queue is blocked by another frame or by a higher-priority queue.
2. **Transmitting:** the credit decreases at `sendSlope`.
3. **Queue empty with positive credit:** the credit is reset to zero. A queue cannot save credit while it has nothing to send.
4. **Queue empty with negative credit:** the credit rises at `idleSlope` back to zero.

Over a long interval the class therefore never uses more than `idleSlope`. Within a short interval it can send a burst only to the extent that it was held back before. That burst is bounded by `hiCredit`.

## 3. The AVB setting

<CommonsFigure
  id="avb-domains"
  caption="Figure 3. AVB domains. Reserved streams can only be carried along paths in which every bridge supports AVB. Boundary ports filter or re-tag the priority of reserved traffic so that it cannot leak into or out of the domain."
/>

AVB defines two shaped stream reservation classes [2]:

| Class | Observation interval | Target worst-case latency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 125 µs | 2 ms over 7 hops |
| B | 250 µs | 50 ms over 7 hops |

Together, the reserved classes may use at most 75 % of the link bandwidth [2]. The `idleSlope` of each class is the sum of the bandwidths reserved by the stream reservation protocol, described in [the stream reservation post](/blog/stream-reservation). Figure 3 shows why a reservation needs every [bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_bridge) on the path: a single non-AVB bridge breaks the guarantee [2].

## 4. Worst-case latency

In [network calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_calculus) terms, a CBS queue offers a rate-latency service with rate `idleSlope`. Its [latency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latency_(engineering)) term grows with the largest frame of lower priority, which may already be on the wire, and with the bursts of higher classes. [The TSN shapers post](/blog/network-calculus-for-tsn) gives the service curve and its residual for lower classes.

A 2026 tutorial on decentralised latency models adds several points that matter in practice [4]:

- **The [802.1BA](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.1BA/4396/) formula is not a bound.** The example latency formula of the AVB profile was shown to be inaccurate. The tutorial derives a network calculus model with formal proofs instead.
- **CBS shapes classes, not streams.** One shaper instance serves the aggregate of all streams in the class. Aggregation adds latency [jitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packet_delay_variation), so the effective burstiness of each stream grows from hop to hop despite the shaping.
- **CBS does not fit a clean shaper-to-shaper split.** Its credit reset depends on whether other frames of the class are still waiting. The shaping step therefore includes queuing and transmission, and each CBS bridge must compute its own queuing delay. With the other shapers studied, the downstream bridge computes the queuing delay of the upstream one. The [ATS post](/blog/asynchronous-traffic-shaping) shows that split.
- **It works best when the egress slope covers the ingress slopes.** CBS performs very well when the `idleSlope` of an egress port is at least the sum of the `idleSlope`s feeding it. When that ratio falls below 1, which happened in almost all of the industrial topologies tested, performance degrades quickly. There, plain strict priority with a network calculus bound was the better choice [4].

A review of [industrial IoT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_internet_of_things) networking places CBS between the other shapers. CBS fits traffic that is regular but occasionally bursty. Scheduled traffic fits regular, critical traffic. Asynchronous shaping fits sporadic critical events [5].

## 5. Summary

| Property | Credit-Based Shaper |
| --- | --- |
| Standard | IEEE 802.1Qav, now in IEEE 802.1Q |
| Acts on | One traffic-class queue at an egress port |
| State | One credit per shaped queue |
| Needs time synchronisation | No |
| Granularity | Per class; all streams of the class share the shaper |
| Main parameter | `idleSlope`, the reserved rate |

## 6. From the articles

Reading notes on Lo Bello and Steiner [6], Yang et al. [7] and Zhao et al. [8], from the copies kept in the repository's [`ressource/`](https://github.com/CNAM-ECE/learning-materials/tree/main/ressource) folder. They expand the margin notes taken while reading.

### Where the delay of a frame comes from

Zero congestion loss rests on computing buffer needs for the **worst case**. Yang et al. [7], after Finn (2018), splits the delay of a packet through one relay into five parts:

<SourceFigure
  id="yang2019-timing-model"
  caption="Figure 4. The TSN timing model: output delay, link delay, preemption delay, processing delay and queuing delay."
/>

1. **Output delay**: from selection for transmission to the first bit on the wire.
2. **Link delay**: propagation on the cable.
3. **Preemption delay**: waiting for an interrupted or non-interruptible frame (see [preemption](/blog/ieee-802-1qbu-frame-preemption)).
4. **Processing delay**: from reception to entry into the output queue.
5. **Queuing delay**: waiting in the output queue. Only this part depends on the other traffic and the shaper, and it is the one that the shaper makes analysable.

Variations in the first four are absorbed by buffers; the fifth is bounded by analysing the queuing algorithm, which is what [network calculus](/blog/network-calculus-for-tsn) does.

### How the shaper works

[IEEE 802.1Qav](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.1Qav/4401/), from the AVB suite, gives real-time guarantees **without** synchronised clocks ([6] §III-B). Each shaped queue has a **credit**:

<SourceFigure
  id="yang2019-cbs-flowchart"
  caption="Figure 5. The CBS decision for one queue. A frame may start only when frames are queued, the credit is non-negative and the port is free."
/>

| Situation | Credit |
| --- | --- |
| Frames waiting, not transmitting (port busy or credit negative) | Increases at the **idleSlope** |
| Transmitting a frame of this queue | Decreases at the **sendSlope** = idleSlope − port rate |
| Queue empty and credit positive | Reset to zero |
| Queue empty and credit negative | Increases back towards zero |

:::caution Correction to the seminar paper
Yang et al. [7] writes that credit *increases when a frame is added to the queue and decreases when a frame is sent*. This is a simplification: credit grows with **time spent waiting** at the idle slope, not with the number of frames enqueued, and it decreases with **time spent sending**. Its own figure (Figure 5) shows the correct rule. Section 2 above gives the exact rules.
:::

The parameters that bound the per-bridge latency are the **idle slope** (the reserved bandwidth), the **maximum frame size** of the class and of interfering lower classes, and the **port rate** ([7] lists them as maximum frame size, maximum reference size and maximum port transmission rate).

### CBS with frame preemption

802.1Qbu specifies how CBS behaves when preemption is on ([6] §IV-C):

- Time during which a preemptable frame of a CBS queue is held or interrupted by express traffic **does not consume credit**: the shaper's *transmit* flag stays false until the frame starts or resumes.
- The extra overhead of preemption (fragment headers) is **not** subtracted from the bandwidth reserved for the shaper.

Without scheduled windows, a CBS on express traffic also limits how long preemptable frames are delayed, which keeps their transit time within the bridge bound.

### AVB traffic in a TSN network

Zhao, Pop, Zheng and Li [8] ask what happens to AVB streams once the same port also carries time-triggered (TT) traffic under a [time-aware shaper](/blog/time-aware-shaper). Their answer is a network calculus analysis of the worst-case delay (WCD) of AVB Class A and Class B, for both ways of protecting TT windows. These notes rewrite the handwritten reading notes on the report.

#### The question and the setting

How should traffic types of different priority share one Ethernet infrastructure, each with a bounded latency? An urgent control signal in a car must not wait behind a video stream, and the video stream must still get its bandwidth.

| Element | Assumption in [8] |
| --- | --- |
| Network | Switched Ethernet, all links at the same rate $C$ (1 Gbit/s, so 1,000 bit/µs), full duplex, multi-hop |
| Routing | Fixed: each flow follows a known sequence of dataflow links (its *dataflow routing*) |
| Topology of flows | **Feed-forward**: no cycle of dependencies between output ports, so bounds can be computed hop by hop |
| Traffic classes | TT (highest), AVB Class A, AVB Class B, BE (lowest); the class of each application is fixed by the designer |
| Flows | TT: frame size and period, scheduled by the GCL. AVB: frame size $l$ and period $p$, giving a token bucket at the source |
| Clocks | All switches and end stations share one time base through IEEE 802.1AS (gPTP); accuracy in the microsecond range is enough to match the windows |
| Scheduling inside a class | Strict priority between classes, FIFO inside a queue, no preemption between AVB classes |
| Analysis | Network calculus: arrival curves and service curves give an upper bound on delay |

<SourceFigure
  id="zhao2017-fig2"
  caption="Figure 6. An output port in [8]: one TT queue, a CBS behind each AVB queue, one BE queue, and a gate per queue driven by the GCL. Transmission selection (TSA) only sees queues whose gate is open."
/>

An AVB frame at the head of its queue may start only when **all three** hold:

1. the gate of its queue is open in the GCL;
2. its CBS credit is zero or positive;
3. no frame of the other AVB class is being sent.

#### Protecting the TT windows

A TT window must start on an idle wire. A frame already being sent when the window opens would delay it, so TSN offers two **integration modes**:

<SourceFigure
  id="zhao2017-fig5"
  caption="Figure 7. The two integration modes. (a) Non-preemption: a guard band keeps the wire idle before the TT frame. (b) Preemption: the AVB frame is cut, the TT frame goes out, and the rest of the AVB frame follows with an overhead."
/>

| | Guard band (non-preemption) | Frame preemption (802.1Qbu / 802.3br) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happens | No new frame may start if it cannot finish before the TT window | A lower-priority frame is interrupted, then resumed after the window |
| Cost | Up to one maximum frame of idle wire before every window: 1,538 bytes on the wire take **12.3 µs** at 1 Gbit/s | A small overhead per interruption: an extra preamble, fragment header and checksum |
| Effect on AVB | Wasted bandwidth and longer AVB delays | Lower AVB delay and better bandwidth use, at the price of some TT jitter |
| Window seen by the analysis | Guard band merged with the TT window: $L^h_{GB+TT,i} = L^h_i + L^h_{GB,i}$ | TT window, then an overhead of duration $T_{OH} = L_{OH}/C$ after it |

The guard band before the $i$-th window is the **smaller** of the transmission time of the largest competing AVB frame and the idle time since the previous window. A short gap between windows therefore gives a short guard band.

<SourceFigure

  caption="Figure 8. Guard bands before the TT windows of one hyperperiod. The guard band before window 1 is cut to the short idle time after window 0."
/>

<SourceFigure

#### CBS inside a gated port

The credit follows the usual rules (section 2), with one addition that the standard left open at the time: **while the gate of an AVB queue is closed, its credit is frozen**. It neither grows during guard bands and TT windows nor is reset. The report adopts this *frozen* behaviour so that the credit cannot overflow while a long TT window keeps AVB frames waiting.

<SourceFigure
  id="zhao2017-fig6"
  caption="Figure 10. Credits of Class A (red) and Class B (blue) while BE, TT and AVB frames share the port. (a) Non-preemption: both credits stay flat during the guard band and the TT window. (b) Preemption: the credits stay flat during the TT window and resume with the overhead."
/>

#### Bounds on the credit

Write $C$ for the link rate, $idSl_M$ for the idle slope of class $M \in \{A, B\}$, $sdSl_M = idSl_M - C < 0$ for its send slope, and $l^{max}_{X}$ for the largest frame of class $X$. The credit of each class stays between two values [8], after Azua and Boyer.

**Lower bound.** The credit is lowest just after the class has sent its largest frame starting from zero credit. Sending takes $l^{max}_{AVB_M}/C$, during which the credit falls at $sdSl_M$:

$$
credit^{min}_M = sdSl_M \cdot \frac{l^{max}_{AVB_M}}{C}.
$$

**Upper bound for Class A.** Class A waits at most for one lower-priority frame already on the wire, a Class B or a BE frame, and gains credit at $idSl_A$ meanwhile:

$$
credit^{max}_A = idSl_A \cdot \frac{l^{max}_n}{C}, \qquad l^{max}_n = \max\left(l^{max}_{AVB_B},\, l^{max}_{BE}\right).
$$

**Upper bound for Class B.** Class B gains credit for as long as it waits, and it waits in three successive steps:

| Step | Who uses the wire | Duration | Credit gained by B |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A BE frame already on the wire | $l^{max}_{BE}/C$ | $idSl_B \cdot l^{max}_{BE}/C$ |
| 2 | Class A sends until its credit $credit^{max}_A$ is used up at the rate $sdSl_A$ | $T^{credit}_A = -\dfrac{l^{max}_n \, idSl_A}{C \, sdSl_A}$ | $idSl_B \cdot T^{credit}_A$ |
| 3 | The last Class A frame, started with credit just above zero | $l^{max}_{AVB_A}/C$ | $idSl_B \cdot l^{max}_{AVB_A}/C$ |

Adding the three steps:

$$
credit^{max}_B = idSl_B \left( \frac{l^{max}_{BE} + l^{max}_{AVB_A}}{C} - \frac{l^{max}_n \, idSl_A}{C \, sdSl_A} \right).
$$

Since $sdSl_A < 0$, the last term is positive: the bound grows with the bandwidth reserved for Class A.

#### When the credit can grow without bound

These bounds hold only if the credit of Class B cannot climb from one peak to a higher one. Figure 11 shows the failure: each time Class A regains the wire, Class B loses less credit than it gained, and its peaks $P_1, P_2, \dots$ keep rising.

<SourceFigure
  id="zhao2017-fig7"
  caption="Figure 11. Credit of Class B rising without bound: the second peak P2 is higher than the first peak P1."
/>

**Theorem 1 (non-overflow condition) [8].** The credit of Class B stays bounded if

$$
sdSl_A \cdot sdSl_B \;\ge\; idSl_A \cdot idSl_B .
$$

Replacing each send slope by $idSl_M - C$ shows what the condition means:

$$
(idSl_A - C)(idSl_B - C) \ge idSl_A \, idSl_B
\;\Longleftrightarrow\;
idSl_A + idSl_B \le C .
$$

The two classes together must not reserve more than the link rate. This is a pre-condition of every AVB bound that follows; AVB configurations in practice stay far below it (75 % of the link at most, section 3).

#### TT windows as an arrival curve

The GCL of output port $h$ repeats every hyperperiod $p^h_{GCL}$, the least common multiple of the periods of the TT flows sharing the port. It contains $N^h$ TT windows; window $j$ lasts $L^h_j$, and $o^h_{j,i}$ is its offset from window $i$ (with $o^h_{i,i} = 0$).

<SourceFigure
  id="zhao2017-fig8"
  caption="Figure 12. The GCL of output port h: three TT windows per hyperperiod, of lengths L0, L1 and L2, and their offsets from window 0."
/>

Taking window $i$ as the starting point, the TT traffic that can leave the port within any interval of length $t$ is at most

$$
\alpha^h_{TT,i}(t) = \sum_{j=i}^{i+N^h-1} L^h_j \cdot C \cdot \left\lceil \frac{t - o^h_{j,i}}{p^h_{GCL}} \right\rceil .
$$

Each term is a staircase: $L^h_j \cdot C$ bits, the most the TT queue can send in window $j$, once per hyperperiod. Since the worst starting window is not known, the TT arrival curve is the upper envelope over all of them (Lemma 1):

$$
\alpha^h_{TT}(t) = \max_{0 \le i \le N^h-1} \alpha^h_{TT,i}(t).
$$

In the non-preemption mode, the same construction with window lengths $L^h_i + L^h_{GB,i}$ gives $\alpha^h_{GB+TT}$ (Lemma 2). Merging each guard band with the window that follows it means the two need not be handled separately, which is possible because the AVB credits are frozen during both.

#### The service left to an AVB class

**Theorem 2 (non-preemption) [8].** The service curve of AVB Class $M$ at output port $h$ is

$$
\beta^{h[npr]}_{AVB_M}(t) = \frac{C \cdot idSl_M}{idSl_M - sdSl_M}
\left[ \sup_{0 \le u \le t} \left\{ u - \frac{\alpha^h_{GB+TT}(u)}{C} - \frac{credit^{max}_M}{idSl_M} \right\} \right]^+ .
$$

How to read it:

- $\alpha^h_{GB+TT}(u)/C$ is the time lost to TT windows and guard bands within $u$;
- $credit^{max}_M / idSl_M$ is a latency: the time for which the class may be blocked while its credit climbs to its maximum;
- the factor in front equals $idSl_M$, because $idSl_M - sdSl_M = C$: what is left is served at the reserved rate.

**Theorem 3 (preemption)** has the same structure, with the TT curve of Lemma 1 instead of $\alpha^h_{GB+TT}$ and an extra term for the preemption overheads that follow the TT windows (Lemma 3).

#### From one hop to the whole path

- **At the source.** An AVB flow sending frames of $l$ bits every $p$ has the token bucket arrival curve $\alpha(t) = l + (l/p)\,t$: burst $b = l$, rate $r = l/p$.
- **At the next hop.** A flow with burst $b$ and rate $r$ that is delayed by at most $d$ leaves with burst $b' = b + r\,d$ and the same rate. This is the output arrival curve of [the fundamental bounds](/blog/three-fundamental-bounds), and it becomes the input of the next port.
- **Feed-forward.** The hop-by-hop order exists because no cycle $p_1 \to p_2 \to \dots \to p_1$ of output ports occurs among the flows.
- **End to end.** The WCD of a flow is the sum of its per-port delays along its route, plus a constant technical latency $d_{tech}$ for each switch crossed.

#### Context from the reading notes

**TAS, which shapes the AVB service here.**

| Advantages | Drawbacks |
| --- | --- |
| Low delay and jitter for TT traffic | Building the GCLs of a network is NP-hard |
| Time-triggered and event-triggered traffic can share the port | The schedule is fixed: adding one flow means computing it again |
| | Sporadic, event-driven critical traffic does not fit a fixed timetable |

TAS controls the TT **queue** through the GCL; it does not schedule each TT flow, and TT frames lose their strict periodicity as they cross the network when end systems are not scheduled.

**ATS, the alternative for sporadic traffic.** [802.1Qcr](/blog/asynchronous-traffic-shaping) reshapes every flow at every hop with a token bucket $\alpha(t) = CBS + CIR \cdot t$: committed burst size $CBS$ (in bits; not the credit-based shaper) and committed information rate $CIR$. Bursts therefore do not accumulate along the path, and no global clock is needed: each switch uses its local time. Le Boudec's result that an interleaved regulator gives this reshaping without increasing the worst-case delay ("shaping for free") is the reason it works; see the [network calculus for TSN post](/blog/network-calculus-for-tsn).
