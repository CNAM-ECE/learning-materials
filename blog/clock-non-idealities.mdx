Everything in network calculus so far assumes that all nodes share one perfect time. In reality every node measures time with its own imperfect clock, and a regulator that enforces "at most $b$ bits plus $r$ per second" measures those seconds locally. This post shows how much the bounds move, and when a regulator stops being safe.

## 1. Clocks are not ideal

| Synchronisation | Example | Timestamping error $\omega$ | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tight | PTP, White Rabbit, GPS | $\approx 10$ ns – 1 µs | Small variation |
| Loose | NTP | $\approx 1$ ms – 1 s | Larger error, weak synchronisation |
| None | Free-running clocks | Unbounded | Intervals measured on one system are still bounded by drift, jitter and wander |

Against an ideal clock $\mathcal{H}_{TAI}$ ($y = t$), a local clock $h_i(t)$ shows four kinds of error:

- **Initial offset**: the time difference between two clocks at the start.
- **Drift**: the accumulated difference from a clock running faster or slower than the reference (a frequency offset).
- **Jitter**: small, rapid variations in time measurements.
- **Wander**: slow variations in clock timing over time.

Regulators use time measurements to decide when a packet may be released, so a non-ideal clock can release a packet too early or hold it too long compared with the real time stamps, and arrival constraints checked with the ideal model are no longer guaranteed.

## 2. The clock model [Thomas 2020]

The model must account for clock errors in three places: when checking that packets conform to arrival constraints; in delay bounds, which may need extra margins; and in regulators, so that clock offsets never make them release packets before their eligibility time.

A time interval measured with clock 1 gives $d$; the same interval measured with clock 2 gives $d'$. Three parameters bound the difference:

- $\omega$, the **time error bound**: about 1 µs in TSN with PTP, $+\infty$ without synchronisation. Synchronisation error: $|d' - d| \le 2\omega$, i.e. $d - 2\omega \le d' \le d + 2\omega$ (each end of the interval can be off by $\omega$).
- $\rho$, the **clock-stability bound**: how much faster or slower one clock can run than another, about $1.0001$ in TSN.
- $\eta$, the **timing-jitter bound**: the additional measurement error caused by jitter, about 2 ns in TSN. Jitter and wander: $d' \le \rho d + \eta$.

Together, the **change-of-clock inequalities**:

$$
\max\left(0,\ \frac{d - \eta}{\rho},\ d - 2\omega\right) \;\le\; d' \;\le\; \min\left(\rho d + \eta,\ d + 2\omega\right).
$$

The model is symmetric: the same inequalities hold with $d$ and $d'$ exchanged. The relative error on delays is in general about $10^{-4}$, negligible, but there are corner cases.

## 3. A token bucket seen from another clock

Two questions:

1. If a flow conforms to a token bucket in one clock, which arrival curve does it have in another clock? Clock differences make traffic **look burstier**, so a regulator cannot always reuse the original curve.
2. How does the lack of synchronisation affect the stability and delay guarantees of regulators?

Suppose the flow satisfies $\alpha^{\mathcal{H}_{Reg}}(t) = rt + b$ in the regulator's clock $\mathcal{H}_{Reg}$ ($r$ the maximum arrival rate, $b$ the maximum burst). Measured with another clock $\mathcal{H}$, an interval of length $t$ may correspond to up to $\min(\rho t + \eta,\ t + 2\omega)$ in $\mathcal{H}_{Reg}$. Substituting, step by step:

$$
\alpha^{\mathcal{H}}(t) = r \cdot \min(\rho t + \eta,\ t + 2\omega) + b = \min\big(\rho r t + b + r\eta,\ rt + b + 2r\omega\big).
$$

- The first term has a larger rate $\rho r$ and a slightly larger burst $b + r\eta$: clock stability and timing jitter make the clocks count time differently.
- The second term keeps the rate $r$ but adds $2r\omega$ to the burst: the synchronisation error at both ends of the interval.

The conversion makes the arrival curve **less tight** (more bursty), although the traffic itself has not changed.

## 4. Non-adapted regulators [Thomas 2020]

A **non-adapted** regulator uses the same nominal arrival curve as at the source, $\alpha(t) = rt + b$, but in its own local clock.

| Clocks | Per-flow regulator (PFR) | Interleaved regulator (IR) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Perfect | Does not increase the worst-case delay | Does not increase the worst-case delay |
| Not synchronised | Unstable: unbounded delay; it may keep delaying traffic to enforce a constraint that no longer matches what it sees | Unstable |
| Synchronised | Delay penalty of at most $4\omega$: the error $2\omega$ at each end of the measured interval accumulates | Still **unstable**, because it uses non-adapted arrival curves; potentially unbounded delay |

An ns-3 simulation (three sources at 147 kb/s, $\omega = 1$ µs, $\rho = 1.0001$) showed the delay through a non-adapted ATS bridge growing by up to 100 µs per second of operation.

**The fix:** the realistic arrival curve must be adapted to the regulator's clock, $\mathcal{H}_{Reg}$. A **rate-and-burst cascade** updates the arrival bounds node by node, with the rate multiplied by $\rho$ and the burst increased by terms in $\eta$ and $\omega$ at every regulator, which keeps PFR and IR stable and the delay guarantees deterministic.
