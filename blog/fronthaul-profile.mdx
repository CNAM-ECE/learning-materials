In a mobile network, the link between a radio unit at the antenna and the equipment that controls it, the fronthaul, used to be a dedicated fibre with a dedicated protocol. IEEE 802.1CM shows how to carry it over ordinary bridged Ethernet, and which TSN features that takes.

## 1. Definition

[IEEE 802.1CM](https://1.ieee802.org/tsn/802-1cm-2018/), *Time-Sensitive Networking for Fronthaul*, defines profiles that select the features, options, configurations, defaults, protocols and procedures of bridges, stations and LANs needed to carry fronthaul streams [1]. Its main points [1]:

- **Interfaces.** It supports fronthaul interfaces such as the eCPRI specification.
- **Partners.** It was developed with the CPRI Cooperation, which contributed the requirements. The synchronisation solutions come from ITU-T Question 13 of Study Group 15.
- **Dates.** It was approved on 7 May 2018 and published on 8 June 2018. Amendment 802.1CMde-2020 adds support for new fronthaul interface, synchronisation and syntonisation standards.
- **Recognition.** It is the first IEEE standard to connect a cellular network's radio equipment to its remote controller through a packet network, in particular a bridged [IEEE 802.3](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.3/10422/) Ethernet network. It earned the IEEE 802.1 working group the 2020 IEEE SA Emerging Technology Award.

It is available free of charge through the IEEE GET Program [1].

## 2. What fronthaul is

<SourceFigure
  id="cm2018-fronthaul"
  caption="Figure 2. 5G fronthaul in its simplest form: the radio base station is split into radio equipment (RE/eRE) at the antenna and radio equipment control (REC/eREC); the link between them is fronthaul, and everything beyond is backhaul."
/>

A radio base station is split into **radio equipment** (RE, or eRE for eCPRI) near the antenna and **radio equipment control** (REC or eREC) further away. Fronthaul is the link between the two. In the overview's words, 5G fronthaul is "everything that is not backhaul" [2].

<SourceFigure
  id="cm2018-splits"
  caption="Figure 3. Functional splits of the base station: no split, the CPRI split below the PHY, and the eCPRI split inside the PHY (PHY-U and PHY-L). Both splits are in the scope of 802.1CM."
/>

Where the split is made decides what crosses the fronthaul. In the **CPRI** split, everything above the radio stays at the controller, and the link carries radio samples. In the **eCPRI** split, part of the physical layer (PHY-L) moves to the radio side, which reduces the bandwidth needed. Both are in the scope of 802.1CM; further splits can be added by amendment [2].

/>

<SourceFigure
  id="cm2018-fronthaul-details"

The design keeps the radio and packet domains apart [2]:

- fronthaul flows are separated, for example by priority and VLAN ID;
- synchronisation is separated from the fronthaul data flows and can be provided by the packet network itself ("sync as a service");
- standard IEEE 802.1/802.3 Ethernet frames enter and leave through edge ports.

## 4. The top-down approach

802.1CM was built from the requirements down [2]:

1. **Requirements**, from the CPRI Cooperation. *Class 1* uses the CPRI split and *Class 2* the eCPRI split, with the same synchronisation requirements for both.
2. **Features** that can meet them: bridging features, TSN features, and synchronisation solutions from ITU-T Q13/15, for example how to use the telecom profile of IEEE 1588.
3. **Profiles** that state exactly how an Ethernet network meets the fronthaul requirements.

## 5. The two profiles

Both profiles are engineered for the worst case and apply to both classes [2]:

| | Profile A | Profile B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Principle | Keep it as simple as possible | Add one simple TSN feature |
| Scheduling | Strict priority | Strict priority + [frame preemption](/blog/ieee-802-1qbu-frame-preemption) ([802.3br](/blog/ieee-802-3br-interspersing-express-traffic) and 802.1Qbu) |
| Fronthaul user data (IQ) | High-priority traffic class | High-priority class, **express** |
| Control and management / other traffic | Lower-priority traffic class | Lower-priority class, **preemptable** |
| Frame size | At most 2000 octets for all traffic | 2000 octets for fronthaul; flexible for other traffic |

The difference is the blocking term of a worst-case analysis. Under Profile A, a fronthaul frame can wait behind one lower-priority frame of up to 2000 octets at every hop, so all traffic must keep its frames short. Under Profile B, preemption reduces that wait to a short fragment. Other traffic can then use large frames without hurting the fronthaul bound. The [802.1Qbu post](/blog/ieee-802-1qbu-frame-preemption) quantifies this blocking.
