Network calculus usually counts bits as a function of time. Writing the same constraints on packet dates instead (when did packet $n$ arrive, when may it leave) is what makes regulators implementable, and it leads to the interleaved regulator that TSN standardised as Asynchronous Traffic Shaping.

## 1. Min-plus or max-plus?

Network calculus mainly uses cumulative functions $R(t)$, the number of bits seen in $t$ time units: that is the min-plus view. Liebeherr uses the cumulative **arrival time** function $T(x)$, the time taken to observe $x$ bits. This flips the time and data axes and gives a dual, quasi-equivalent approach in max-plus algebra, linked by pseudo-inverses $R = T^{\downarrow}$, $T = R^{\uparrow}$:

$$
f^{\downarrow}(x) = \inf\{t : f(t) \ge x\}, \qquad f^{\uparrow}(x) = \sup\{t : f(t) \le x\}.
$$

**Example.** For $f(t) = b\lceil t/\tau \rceil$: $f^{\uparrow}(x) = \tau\lfloor x/b \rfloor$ for $x \ge 0$, and $f^{\downarrow}(x) = \tau\lceil x/b - 1 \rceil$ for $x > 0$.

**Chang's max-plus calculus** uses the daters (a marked point process) with a convolution that is neither associative nor commutative,

$$
(A \odot_L g)(n) = \max_{1 \le m \le n}\big(A_m + g(\ell_m + \dots + \ell_{n-1})\big).
$$

A flow is **$g$-regular** if $A \ge A \odot_L g$. With $g(x) = x/r$ this is $A_n - A_{n-1} \ge \ell_{n-1}/r$, the LRQ rule of Specht and Samii: a constraint between two consecutive inter-arrival times. $g$-regularity is not equivalent to an arrival curve and is hard to use with aggregation, which is where min-plus is strong; it maps only approximately:

$$
g(\ell) = \frac{\ell}{r} \Rightarrow \alpha(t) = rt + \ell^{MAX}, \qquad \alpha(t) = rt + \ell^{MAX} \Rightarrow g(\ell) = \frac{(\ell - \ell^{MAX})^+}{r}.
$$

## 2. Arrival curves on packet dates

**Theorem** (Le Boudec 2018). With $A_n$ the arrival date of packet $n$, the arrival curve constraint $\alpha$ is equivalent to

$$
A_n - A_m \ge \alpha^{\downarrow}(\ell_m + \dots + \ell_n) \qquad \text{for all } 1 \le m \le n,
$$

using the pseudo-inverse $\alpha^{\downarrow}$. For a token bucket, $\alpha^{\downarrow}(x) = \max\left(0, \frac{x - b}{r}\right)$.

| | Original definition | Equivalent max-plus formulation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Affine (leaky bucket) | $R(t) - R(s) \le r(t - s) + b$ | $A_n - A_m \ge \dfrac{L_m + \dots + L_n - b}{r}$ |
| Staircase ($b$ bits per $\tau$) | $R(t) - R(s) \le b\left\lceil \dfrac{t-s}{\tau} \right\rceil$ | $A_n - A_m \ge \tau\left\lceil \dfrac{L_m + \dots + L_n - b}{b} \right\rceil$ |

In the leaky-bucket row the right-hand side is a latency: how long the packets $m..n$ must be spread over. The staircase form is the one useful for TSN.

**Rate-latency service.** A FIFO element offering $\beta_{R,T}$ to a packetized input satisfies, for every $n$, for some $m \le n$:

$$
D_n \le A_m + \frac{\ell_m + \dots + \ell_n}{R} + T ,
$$

departure time $\le$ an arrival time plus the time to serve the packets since then plus the latency. Conversely, an element with this property is a rate-latency element followed by a packetizer (a guaranteed-rate node).

## 3. The packetized greedy shaper in the time domain

The output times $D^*_n$ of the packetized greedy shaper are given recursively, with a discrete index:

$$
D^*_1 = A_1, \qquad D^*_n = D^*_{n-1} \vee A_n \vee \bigvee_{m=1}^{n-1}\Big(A_m + \alpha^{\downarrow}(\ell_m + \dots + \ell_n)\Big).
$$

A packet leaves no earlier than the previous one (FIFO), no earlier than it arrives, and no earlier than the arrival curve allows. For any other packetized shaper, $D'_n \ge D^*_n$: the greedy one is optimal.

## 4. The interleaved regulator

An **interleaved regulator** (IR) is a single FIFO queue for several flows (a bank of packetizers interleaved in one queue) whose output gives every flow $f$ its own arrival curve $\alpha_f$. **Theorem:** there is one minimal interleaved regulator, which outputs every packet no later than any other.

$$
D_n = \max\left\{A_n,\ D_{n-1},\ \bigvee_{m=1}^{i-1}\Big(A^f_m + \alpha^{\downarrow}_f\big(\ell^f_m + \dots + \ell^f_i\big)\Big)\right\},
$$

where packet $n$ is the $i$-th packet of its flow $f$. The last term is the eligibility time of the packet at the head of the queue.

- One FIFO queue for all packets of all flows.
- The packet at the head is examined and held until it can be released without violating the arrival curve of its flow.
- The other packets wait for their turn.

This is the urgency-based scheduler of Specht and Samii, called **Asynchronous Traffic Shaping** in IEEE TSN ([802.1Qcr](/blog/asynchronous-traffic-shaping)).

**Theorem: the minimal IR does not increase worst-case delay.** Flows with arrival curves $\alpha_f$ cross a FIFO system $S$ and then a minimal IR with the same $\alpha_f$. With $A_n$ the arrival at $S$, $D_n$ the exit of $S$ and $E_n$ the exit of the IR:

$$
\sup_n (D_n - A_n) = \sup_n (E_n - A_n).
$$

*Proof with a damper.* Let $d$ be the worst-case delay of $S$ across all flows. Replace the IR by a damper that delays every packet to exactly $d$ after its arrival at $S$ (causal since $d$ is at least the worst case). Its output is the input shifted by $d$, so every flow keeps $\alpha_f$ and the order is kept: the damper is an interleaved regulator. By minimality, the regulated output is never later than the damped one.

## 5. Other regulation constraints

TSN defines source constraints per packet as well as per bit. **Packet burstiness** $PB(\rho, K)$: at most $\rho t + K$ packets in any interval of duration $t$, equivalently

$$
A_n - A_m \ge \frac{n - m + 1 - K}{\rho} \qquad \text{for } m \le n .
$$

Regulators generalise shapers: under mild conditions ("$\Pi$-regulation"), any regulation rule has a minimal regulator and any combination of rules has a minimal interleaved regulator. The minimal regulator does not increase per-flow delay; the minimal interleaved regulator does not increase the delay of a FIFO system.

**Packet count and event streams.** With $R(t)$ the bits up to $t$, $P(d)$ the number of packets in $d$ bits (a relation from bits to packets) and $E(t)$ the number of packets up to $t$, $E(t) = P(R(t))$ and $P^L(R(t)) = P^{\downarrow}(P(R(t)))$. The conditions $E(t) - E(s) \le f(t - s)$ and $A_n - A_m \ge f^{\downarrow}(n - m + 1)$ are equivalent. In real-time systems the same tool counts the CPU cycles that job arrivals need, and superposition gives the delay analysis.

## 6. Regulators against cascading burstiness

From the LINCS 2023 notes [3]: a regulator, a shaper that delays packets to keep burstiness within a prescribed arrival curve, is typically placed at the network edge to protect against misbehaving sources. Inside a per-class network it limits the cascade of [the stability post](/blog/fifo-networks-and-stability), where a flow's burstiness grows as $b' = b + rJ$ with $J$ its delay-jitter bound, and it can break cyclic dependencies (the AFDX network is an example). The price is a burstiness–latency trade-off: the regulator holds back part of the packets to respect the token bucket, which may add delay to some packets, but never to the worst case.
