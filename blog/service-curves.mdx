The arrival curve bounds the demand; the **service curve** bounds the supply. It abstracts a scheduler, a link or a whole [switch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_switch) into a single function that lower-bounds the service it delivers.

## 1. Min-plus service curve

A system with input $R$ and output $R^*$ offers a (min-plus) **service curve** $\beta$ if

$$
R^* \ge R \otimes \beta, \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad R^*(t) \ge \inf_{0 \le s \le t} \{\, R(s) + \beta(t - s) \,\}.
$$

Read operationally: for every $t$ there is some instant $s \le t$ such that the system has delivered at least $\beta(t - s)$ bits since $s$ on top of everything that arrived before $s$. The definition mirrors the impulse response of linear systems: an ideal pipe with [latency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latency_(engineering)) $T$ has service curve $\delta_T$, a constant-rate link of rate $C$ has $\lambda_C$.

## 2. Strict service curve

A system offers a **strict service curve** $\beta$ if, during any *backlogged period* of duration $u$ (an interval in which the system is never empty), the output is at least $\beta(u)$.

Every strict service curve is a min-plus service curve; the converse is false. The distinction matters because several results, in particular blind multiplexing in [the multiplexing post](/blog/aggregate-multiplexing-residual-service), require the strict version. Schedulers that serve as soon as they are backlogged, possibly after a bounded interruption, are naturally described by strict service curves.

## 3. Rate-latency servers

The workhorse of the theory is the **rate-latency** function

$$
\beta_{R,T}(t) = R\,[t - T]^+ = \max(0,\, R(t - T)).
$$

It models a server that, after a worst-case start-up latency $T$, serves at rate at least $R$. Many real schedulers fall into this class, for example:

| System | Rate $R$ | Latency $T$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Link of rate $C$ with processing delay $t_{proc}$ | $C$ | $t_{proc}$ |
| Non-preemptive link, highest priority, max lower-priority frame $L_{lo}$ | $C$ | $L_{lo}/C$ |
| [Generalised Processor Sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_processor_sharing) with weight $\phi_i$ | $\phi_i C / \sum_j \phi_j$ | $0$ |

Rate-latency functions are convex, so by the concatenation rule of [the min-plus algebra post](/blog/min-plus-algebra) a tandem of rate-latency servers is again rate-latency.

## 4. Example: a Time-Aware Shaper window

Consider an egress queue controlled by an [802.1Qbv](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.1Qbv/6068/) gate that is open for $w$ time units in every cycle of length $c$, on a link of rate $C$. Assume, for simplicity, that no other queue transmits during this window and ignore the guard band that prevents frames from overrunning the window.

In the worst case, the queue becomes backlogged just as its gate closes. It then waits $c - w$, is served at rate $C$ for $w$, waits again, and so on. The resulting cumulative service is a staircase whose lower corners lie on the line of slope $Cw/c$ passing through $(c - w,\, 0)$. Hence the queue receives the strict rate-latency service curve

$$
\beta(t) = \frac{C w}{c}\, \bigl[\, t - (c - w) \,\bigr]^+ .
$$

The rate is the reserved share of the link; the latency is the longest closed interval. Accounting for the guard band and for non-preemptive blocking increases the latency and slightly decreases the effective window.

:::tip[Modelling choice]
The rate-latency bound discards the staircase structure and is therefore pessimistic for traffic that is *synchronised* with the gate schedule. Time-triggered [TSN](https://1.ieee802.org/tsn/) analyses often exploit that synchronisation explicitly; [network calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_calculus) remains the method of choice for traffic that is not synchronised, and for the residual service left to lower-priority classes.
:::

## 5. Service curves are a contract, not a description

A service curve is a guarantee that the system *at least* provides, not an exact model. This has two consequences that recur in the rest of the series.

1. **Composability.** Any lower bound of a service curve is still a service curve, so one may always simplify a complex curve to a rate-latency one and keep sound (if looser) bounds.
2. **Robustness.** Bounds derived from service curves remain valid under any implementation that honours the curve, which is what a certificate needs.

### What a service curve says

An arrival curve constrains the traffic that may enter the network; a service curve is a **lower bound on the service** a network element provides to a flow, a minimum service guarantee.

$$
R^* \ge R \otimes \beta \iff \forall t \ge 0,\ \exists s \in [0, t] : R^*(t) \ge R(s) + \beta(t - s),
$$

with $\beta : \mathbb{R}^+ \to \mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty\}$, wide-sense increasing and $\beta(0) = 0$. The output $R^*$ is above the lower envelope of all curves $R(s) + \beta(t - s)$. $\beta(0) = 0$ means that no service is promised at time 0.

**Proposition 1.3.1 (removing the infimum).** If $\beta$ is continuous, there is a specific $t_0 \in [0, t]$, a point where the infimum is reached, with $R^*(t) \ge R(t_0) + \beta(t - t_0)$. **Proposition 1.3.2.** If $\beta$ is also convex, $t_0$ can be chosen as a non-decreasing function of $t$.

### Strict and non-strict

| | Strict service curve | (Min-plus) service curve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Condition | For every $s < t$ inside a backlogged period ($R^*(u) < R(u)$ on $(s, t]$): $R^*(t) - R^*(s) \ge \beta(t - s)$ | $R^* \ge R \otimes \beta$ |
| Meaning | A guarantee on the amount the server is certain to have served whenever the flow has data waiting | A weaker guarantee that holds only in the min-plus sense |
| Typical system | A single queuing point | Any system, including a chain of nodes |

Strict implies non-strict. *Continuously backlogged* means there is always data of the flow waiting during the whole interval. A server can be busy without serving the flow under consideration, for example while it serves another flow: that is why the strict guarantee is the useful one for a single queue.

### Constant-rate server: $\beta(t) = ct$, step by step

1. Take $s$ = the beginning of the busy period that contains $t$: the most recent time in the past when the queue was empty.
2. At $s$ the queue is empty, so output equals input: $R^*(s) = R(s)$.
3. During $(s, t]$ the queue is never empty, so the server works at rate $c$: $R^*(t) - R^*(s) = c\,(t - s)$.
4. Substituting step 2: $R^*(t) - R(s) = c\,(t - s)$, i.e. $R^*(t) \ge R(s) + \beta(t - s)$ with $\beta(t) = ct$.

$\beta(t) = ct$ is a service curve, and also a strict one.

### Guaranteed-delay node: $\delta_T$

$$
\delta_T(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & t \le T \\ +\infty & t > T \end{cases}, \qquad (R \otimes \delta_T)(t) = R(t - T).
$$

For a node that is FIFO for the flow, **delay $\le T$ $\iff$ the node offers the service curve $\delta_T$**: $R^*(t) \ge R(t - T)$, i.e. a bit leaves at most $T$ after it arrived. Example: a *server with vacations*, which serves packets on the line but whose scheduler may be busy doing something else for up to $T$. EDF schedulers are analysed this way: as long as the flow has backlog, the node keeps sending, and every bit leaves within its deadline $T$.

### Rate-latency and static priority

$$
\beta_{R,T}(t) = R\,(t - T)^+ = \max\big(0,\ R(t - T)\big).
$$

**Static priority without preemption**, line rate $C$:

- **High priority:** $\beta_H(t) = (Ct - MTU_L)^+$, strict. A high-priority packet can arrive while a low-priority packet is being sent; without preemption it waits for at most one low-priority packet, $MTU_L$.
- **Low priority**, when high priority is constrained by $\alpha(t) = rt + b$ with $r < C$:
  $$\beta_L(t) = \big((C - r)t - b\big)^+ \quad \text{(not strict)}, \qquad \beta'_L(t) = \big((C - r)t - b - MTU_L\big)^+ \quad \text{(strict)}.$$
  The burst $b$ is the worst-case interruption by high-priority traffic; $C - r$ is what is left once high priority takes its rate, and $r < C$ ensures that high priority does not saturate the server, so low priority is not starved.

**Deficit Round Robin** (processor sharing, as in multitask systems): one queue per flow, each visited in turn and served up to $Q_i$ bits. It offers flow $i$ the strict service curve $\beta_i(t) = R_i(t - T_i)^+$ with

$$
R_i = \frac{Q_i}{\sum_j Q_j}\,c, \qquad T_i = \frac{\bar Q_i + \bar L_i}{c} + L_{\max,i}\left(\frac{1}{R_i} - \frac{1}{c}\right), \qquad \bar Q_i = \sum_{j \ne i} Q_j,\ \bar L_i = \sum_{j \ne i} L_{\max,j}.
$$

Service-curve characterisations exist for most schedulers used in time-sensitive networks: CBS, DRR, IEEE AVB, the time-aware shaper.

### GPS as a service curve

A Generalised Processor Sharing node guarantees flow $i$ a rate $r$ during any busy period of the flow. Starting the busy period at $t_0$, when $R(t_0) = R^*(t_0)$:

$$
R^*(t) - R^*(t_0) \ge r\,(t - t_0) \;\Rightarrow\; R^*(t) \ge R(t_0) + r(t - t_0) \ge \inf_{0 \le s \le t}\{R(s) + r(t - s)\},
$$

so $R^* \ge R \otimes \gamma_{r,0}$ with $\gamma_{r,0}(t) = rt$.
