The Time-Aware Shaper ([TAS](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.1Qbv/6068/)) is the [TSN](https://1.ieee802.org/tsn/) tool that gives the strongest timing guarantees, and the hardest one to configure. The mechanism in the [bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_bridge) is simple: a gate per queue and a timetable. The difficulty lies in computing the timetable.

## 1. Definition

**Scheduled traffic**, [IEEE 802.1Qbv](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.1Qbv/6068/) (now part of [IEEE 802.1Q](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.1Q/10323/)), adds a **transmission gate** to each queue of an egress port [1][3]:

- a queue whose gate is **closed** is ignored by transmission selection;
- a queue whose gate is **open** competes as usual, under strict priority or a shaper.

The gate states follow a **gate control list (GCL)**. Each entry holds the open/closed state of every gate and a duration. The list repeats with a fixed **cycle time**, from a **base time** expressed in the synchronised time of [802.1AS](/blog/ieee-802-1as-time-synchronization). The result is a time-division schedule on each link, with windows reserved for given priorities [2].

The GCL is built centrally. In the configuration model of [802.1Qcc](https://1.ieee802.org/tsn/802-1qcc/), the CNC computes a schedule for the whole network and installs a list on every bridge [4]. The [SDN post](/blog/sdn-and-tsn-configuration) describes that controller.

## 2. Slices, infringement and the guard band

<CommonsFigure
  id="qbv-schedule"
  caption="Figure 2. An 802.1Qbv schedule. Each cycle starts with time slice 1, reserved for VLAN priority 3, followed by time slice 2 for all other priorities."
/>

In Figure 2, frames of priority 3 never wait behind other traffic. During slice 1 their queue is the only one with an open gate. But the gate only decides when a frame may **start**. A frame that has already started is not stopped when its gate closes.

<CommonsFigure
  id="qbv-infringement"
  caption="Figure 3. Time slice infringement. A best-effort frame that starts late in slice 2 is still being sent when slice 1 of the next cycle begins, and delays the time-critical traffic."
/>

The remedy is a **guard band**: before each protected slice, no new frame of the other queues may start. To be safe the guard band must be as long as the largest interfering frame. For a full-size [Ethernet](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.3/10422/) frame with preamble and inter-frame gap, that is about 12 µs at 1 Gbit/s and about 123 µs at 100 Mbit/s.

<CommonsFigure
  id="qbv-guard-band"
  caption="Figure 4. The same schedule with a guard band (G) at the end of each slice 2. The link stays idle during G unless a frame short enough to finish before slice 1 can be sent."
/>

The guard band is bandwidth lost on every cycle. [Frame preemption](/blog/ieee-802-1qbu-frame-preemption) reduces it from a whole frame to a small fragment.

## 3. What a schedule has to know

<TasModelsFigure caption="Figure 5. The inputs of a TAS scheduling problem. A study of 17 methods sorts them into network, traffic and scheduling models. Each method picks one option per dimension, so two methods often solve different problems." />

A 2023 systematic review and experimental study of TAS scheduling [4] starts from a precise statement of what a scheduler is given. Some of the inputs are practical constraints that are easy to forget:

- **GCL length is bounded.** Bridges support only a limited number of entries, typically between 8 and 1024.
- **Priority-to-queue mapping may differ from bridge to bridge**, so the same stream can sit in different queues along its path.
- **Traffic models differ.** A *fully schedulable* model lets the scheduler choose the release time of each stream at the talker. A *partially schedulable* model takes release times as given.
- **Delay models differ.** In a *no-wait* schedule, frames are forwarded without queuing at any hop. *Wait-allowed* schedules let frames wait in egress queues and have a larger solution space. *Window-based* models reserve windows for groups of streams and allow some [jitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packet_delay_variation).
- **Queue isolation.** If two streams share a queue, the FIFO order must match the schedule. Otherwise a lost or delayed frame shifts the others. Methods enforce FIFO, frame-based or stream-based isolation, with stream-based the strictest.

The 17 methods then split along three lines: fixed routing or joint routing and scheduling, no-wait or wait-allowed, and exact solutions ([SMT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisfiability_modulo_theories) or [ILP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_programming) solvers) or heuristics [4].

## 4. Lessons from the experimental study

The study ran all 17 methods on the same stream sets, in simulation and on an eight-bridge testbed [4]. Its main conclusions:

1. **No method dominates.** Each method wins only under some settings. Comparisons in earlier papers were often valid only for the setting they used.
2. **Simple models scale better.** No-wait models and simple heuristics, such as list scheduling, handled large workloads better than exact formulations.
3. **Joint routing and scheduling can backfire.** On large networks it can do worse than fixed routing, because the problem becomes too large to solve.
4. **Flexibility at the talker pays more than flexibility in the bridges.** Letting the scheduler choose release times helped more than letting frames wait in bridges.
5. **With few queues, prefer no-wait.**
6. **Real hardware adds constraints the models omit.** These include collisions between scheduled frames and [PTP](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/1588/6825/) messages, a minimum gap between frames at end stations (about 50 µs on the testbed, far more than in bridges), and the time granularity of GCL entries.

## 5. Where TAS fits

TAS can isolate a critical stream completely from all other traffic. A review of industrial technologies notes that a well-configured TAS gives extremely low delay variation and a transmission delay bound of the order of 100 µs. It also notes that a small time error can desynchronise the windows and delay scheduled traffic by a whole cycle, and that TAS requires central management [5]. A 2026 tutorial on decentralised reservation leaves TAS out of its models for that reason. It adds that a lowest-latency schedule is not the same as a schedule that admits the most streams, because TAS reserves the link for a stream even when that stream sends nothing [6].

For worst-case analysis, a queue behind a gate sees a service that is zero while the gate is closed and the full link rate while it is open. [The service curves post](/blog/service-curves) derives that service curve, and [the TSN shapers post](/blog/network-calculus-for-tsn) uses it.

## 7. From the articles

Reading notes on Lo Bello and Steiner [7], Yang et al. [8] and Xue et al. [4], from the copies kept in the repository's [`ressource/`](https://github.com/CNAM-ECE/learning-materials/tree/main/ressource) folder. They expand the margin notes taken while reading.

### The mechanism

[IEEE 802.1Qbv](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.1Qbv/6068/) brings **time-triggered** communication into 802.1. Its particular choice is to schedule **queues, not frames** ([7] §IV-B):

- Each queue has a **gate**, open or closed. Transmission selection only considers queues whose gate is open.
- A **gate control list (GCL)** says when each gate opens and closes. It repeats with a **cycle time** and has a limited number of entries (typically 8 to 1024, depending on the bridge, Xue et al. [4] §II-A).
- The standard actually stores relative durations between gate changes; Lo Bello and Steiner [7] writes them as absolute times for clarity.
- End stations must implement the same gates, but at their interface, frame-level scheduling cannot be told apart from gate scheduling.
- The network-wide schedule is computed by a **CNC** ([4] §I).

**Guard band.** A queue must stop starting new frames early enough that the last one finishes before its gate closes; otherwise it would delay the queue opening at that instant. This interval is the **guard band**. A simple bridge reserves one maximum frame time; a better one checks whether the next frame still fits. Windows protected this way are called **protected windows**.

### The example in Lo Bello and Steiner [7]

ES1 is synchronised and time-critical; ES3 is not synchronised and sends best-effort traffic; both send to ES2 through bridge B1.

  caption="Figure 6. Without Qbv (top), a frame of ES3 that becomes ready slightly earlier is sent first and delays the frame of ES1, even with a lower priority. With Qbv (bottom), the gate of queue 4 closes before the frame of ES1 is ready, so ES1 is never delayed."
/>

Priorities alone cannot keep ES3 from delaying ES1, because a low-priority frame already on the wire cannot be stopped. A GCL entry that closes queue 4 (ES3) in time, while queue 7 (ES1) is open, solves it.

### Computing the GCL

The gate mechanism is simple; finding the gate times is not. Schedule synthesis is in general **NP-complete** ([7]). Two families of methods exist: dedicated search (heuristics, meta-heuristics, genetic algorithms) and general solvers (ILP, SMT). Xue et al. [4] reviews them with three groups of models:

| Model group | Choices | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Network** | Propagation and processing delay, line rate, queues for TT traffic (≤ 8), maximum GCL length, synchronisation error | Constraints that a schedule must respect on real hardware |
| **Traffic** | Fully or partially schedulable release times; strictly or non-strictly periodic; payload over several frames; implicit, constrained or arbitrary deadline; zero-jitter or bounded jitter | What the application allows the scheduler to choose |
| **Scheduling** | **No-wait** (no queuing along the path) or **wait-allowed**; frame or stream isolation in queues; joint routing; fragmentation; preemption | The search space and the delay and jitter obtained |

### What the 17-method comparison found

Xue et al. [4] ran 17 TAS scheduling methods (2016–2022) in simulation and on an eight-bridge testbed:

<SourceFigure
  id="xue2023-fig6"
  caption="Figure 8. The testbed: eight TTTech evaluation boards in a ring, Raspberry Pi end stations with Intel i210 NICs, and a CNC and CUC."
/>

- **No method wins everywhere.** Each model or method is best only under some settings.
- **Settings bias conclusions.** Results depend heavily on the stream sets, topologies and resource limits chosen. Use broad settings, or realistic ones taken from profiles such as [IEC/IEEE 60802](/blog/iec-ieee-60802-industrial-profile).
- **Metrics can disagree.** The schedulable ratio and a pairwise schedulability advantage gave inconsistent rankings; several metrics, with statistically sound data, are needed.
- **Describe the experiment fully**: model assumptions, solver settings, stream specifications, and exactly where delay is measured.
- **Model assumptions hold on hardware.** Propagation delay (a few ns), processing delay (≤ 1.9 µs per hop) and synchronisation error can be bounded on the testbed, which justifies the constant-or-bounded assumptions of most methods.
- **Constraints often ignored**: the maximum GCL length of real bridges, and the limited number of queues.

### The scheduling models in detail

| Choice | Options | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scheduling entity** | Frame (one GCL entry per frame, no interference); window (frames share an entry, causing jitter; window assigned, partially or not); class (asynchronous, out of scope) | Frame-based gives determinism; window-based gives short GCLs |
| **Queue isolation** | Unrestricted (as in TTEthernet; ignores FIFO order, so the schedule may deviate); explicit: FIFO order kept, **frame isolation** (one frame of a stream in the queue at a time, robust to loss) or **stream isolation** (stricter) | Only wait-allowed models are affected |
| **Routing** | **FR**: fixed routes, usually shortest paths; **JRS**: joint routing and scheduling | JRS schedules more but costs more |
| **Fragmentation versus preemption** | FRAG: schedule-aware fragmentation cuts store-and-forward delay but adds headers; PRE: express interrupts preemptable | PRE gives no delay gain over FRAG (reassembly) but saves bandwidth |
| **Non-overlap constraint** | Big-M (ILP), disjunction (SMT), AllDifferent (CP) | CP is faster than SMT and ILP |

A maximum Ethernet frame is 1,522 bytes, including a 22-byte header with the VLAN tag. Payloads above 1,500 bytes are split into frames, scheduled one by one or back to back as a block.

**The 17 methods.**

| Class | Methods |
| --- | --- |
| FR, no-wait | SMT-NW (job-shop ILP with guard-band compression and tabu), SMT-FRAG (SMT with fragments), DT (divisibility theory, incremental search without backtracking) |
| FR, wait-allowed | SMT-WA (first queue isolation in TSN), AT (array theory for windows), I-OMT (iterative OMT by groups), CP-WA (constraint programming with decomposition), SMT-PRE (SMT choosing express or preemptable), LS-TB (list scheduling with backtracking on conflict sets), LS-PL (per-link phases) |
| JRS | JRS-WA (first joint ILP, prunes infeasible paths), JRS-MC (multicast), JRS-NW and JRS-NW-L (ILP with indicator constraints, speed-ups), LS (list scheduling over routes, no backtracking), I-ILP (iterative ILP with grouping and multicast routing), CG (conflict-graph independent sets, heuristic plus ILP) |

Other objectives in the literature: minimum delay and jitter, minimum number of queues, better QoS for coexisting BE or AVB traffic, reliability (retransmission, disjoint routes, FRER, out-of-sync tolerance), **online reconfiguration** (adding and removing streams, two-stage updates that avoid deadlock), and learning-based schedulers (kNN, GCN with DRL), which give no determinism guarantee.

**Testbed measurements.**

| Quantity | Measured | Used in simulation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Propagation delay (RTT on a CAT7 cable) | 2–6 ns | 6 ns |
| Processing delay (inferred) | ≤ 1.9 µs | 1.9 µs |
| Synchronisation error, after grandmaster election (about 5 s) | ≤ 10 ns | 10 ns |
| System clock to PTP hardware clock offset | ≤ 50 ns | 50 ns |

16 of the 17 methods ran on the hardware (SMT-FRAG produced fragments too small for the hardware windows). On a validation set of 8 streams with 1 ms periods and 10,000 frames, the **measured delay never exceeded the analysed worst case**.

**Simulation set-up.** 10 to 220 streams (220 is above industrial utilisation bounds); periods from 50 µs to 4 ms in six types, so the cycle is their LCM; five payload types; line, ring, tree and mesh topologies of 8 to 78 bridges (the 802.1AS hop limit); 1 Gbit/s; 8 queues all for TT traffic. 38,400 instances, each with a 2 h and 4 GB limit; Python with Z3 and Gurobi. Two metrics: the **schedulable ratio** (unknown counted as unschedulable, so a lower bound) and the pairwise **schedulability advantage** on known results.

**Findings.**

1. **F1. Complex models help in theory, their overhead hurts in practice.** JRS beats FR on advantage but its ratio drops by 41.8 % against 10.9 % as the size grows, worse on rings and meshes. FRAG/PRE have the best advantage (~14 %) but schedule under 10 % beyond 70 streams. Wait-allowed beats no-wait on advantage; no-wait beats wait-allowed under load. Full schedulability beats partial: choosing release times at the end stations matters more than at the bridges. Frame-based beats window-based because GCL length limits windows.
2. **F2. No global best.** Exact methods win below about 100 streams, heuristics above, and heuristics exploit complex models better (CG over JRS-NW, I-OMT over I-ILP).
3. **F3. Workload hurts scalability more than network size.** Runtime and memory grow with the number of streams; more bridges give longer but sparser paths. This matters because the CNC is often embedded.
4. **F4. Schedule quality varies.** Frame-based GCLs can exceed 2,000 entries, while hardware supports at most 1,024; window-based and JRS schedules are shorter. Delay grows with bridges (92 to 192 µs); no-wait is lower and JRS higher. Jitter *falls* with load because windows get tighter. Unrestricted queue isolation uses too many queues.

**Constraints that real hardware adds.** PTP frames can collide with TT frames; an end station needs an inter-frame gap of about 50 µs; GCL windows have a granularity. The practical choice: simple or heuristic methods under heavy load, avoid JRS at scale, no-wait when few queues are available.

**Threats to validity** named by the authors: model, algorithm and implementation effects are not separated; only two window-based methods; some implementations are proofs of concept; parameters not tuned. Future work: avionics and automotive instances, fault tolerance, an open dataset.

### Scalability and jitter

- Scheduling scales poorly in theory, but recent solvers handle thousands of messages ([7] §VI).
- With Qbv, jitter can be reduced to the precision of [clock synchronisation](/blog/ieee-802-1as-time-synchronization). A clock failure, though, makes the gates switch at the wrong times.
