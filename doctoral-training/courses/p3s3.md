# P3S3 — Privacy, Protocols & Proofs

An international five-day summer school for doctoral candidates and early-career researchers, held at Institut Pascal in Orsay. It combines advanced cryptography, formal security analysis and privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs), with lectures and a research poster session.

## What the course covers

- **Cryptographic protocols:** zero-knowledge proofs, homomorphic encryption and electronic voting; constructions including Schnorr proofs, IOP/PIOP and polynomial commitments.
- **Privacy-enhancing technologies:** technical and legal perspectives, including the trade-offs among privacy, utility and computational cost.
- **Formal security methods:** protocol verification using Tamarin; information-flow analysis, including static analysis, dynamic monitoring, non-interference and preservation through compilation.
- **Legal context:** GDPR and European digital identity wallets.
- **Research communication:** presenting a poster to an international, interdisciplinary audience.

The programme develops skills in evaluating modern privacy technologies, specifying and formally analysing security properties, situating technical choices in their legal context, and communicating research. Participants can connect these methods to their own research questions.

## 1. Security protocol analysis: provable guarantees for apps

**In short.** Symbolic tools such as Tamarin find real attacks because they analyse whole protocols, but their classic models of cryptography are too ideal: signatures and Diffie–Hellman have subtle properties that real attacks exploit. Signal shows what modern protocols must guarantee, up to healing after a compromise.

### Symbolic analysis

- **Symbolic** (Dolev–Yao) analysis checks a protocol against an attacker who controls the network, with cryptography treated as perfect black boxes. Modern tools such as Tamarin specify and check such problems; they have been applied to voting protocols, IEEE 802.11 (Wi-Fi), TLS 1.3, EMV payment cards, Apple iMessage PQ3 and SPDM.

<SourceFigure
  id="cremers2026-abstraction"
  caption="Figure 1. Abstraction levels: reality in full detail, the computational model, which keeps details where proofs need them, and the symbolic model, which abstracts most of them to analyse whole protocols."
/>

- **Abstraction** is the trade-off. Real details, such as key lengths and some schemes, belong to the computational model; the symbolic level abstracts them away so that a large part of the system can be analysed: the practical view.
- In Tamarin a protocol is a set of **terms** (messages) and **facts** (sticky notes on the fridge), with special facts $\mathrm{Fr}(t)$, $\mathrm{In}(t)$, $\mathrm{Out}(t)$ and $K(t)$ (the attacker knows $t$). The state of the system is a multiset of facts, starting empty, and **rules** are the transitions.

### Signatures: what the abstraction misses

A signature scheme has three algorithms:

| Algorithm | Input → output |
| --- | --- |
| $\mathsf{KeyGen}$ | → key pair $(sk, pk)$; $pk$ is public |
| $\mathsf{Sign}(sk, m)$ | → signature $\sigma$ |
| $\mathsf{Verify}(\sigma, m, pk)$ | → true or false |

The usual guarantee is **unforgeability**: an adversary who adaptively asks for signatures on messages of its choice cannot produce a valid signature on a message it never submitted. It says nothing about **maliciously generated key pairs**, which affect few people but break protocols that assume more. Modern protocols keep at least one trusted party and must still survive:

- **Duplicate signature key selection (DSKS):** given $(\sigma, m, pk)$, the attacker builds a new key pair $(sk', pk')$ such that $\mathsf{Verify}(\sigma, m, pk') = \text{true}$: the same signature verifies under the attacker's key.
- **Key substitution:** $(\sigma, m, pk) \to (\sigma, m, pk')$.
- **Message-key substitution:** $(\sigma, m, pk) \to (\sigma, m', pk')$, with a maliciously generated key.
- **Small subgroups** in Diffie–Hellman: sending an element of small order instead of an element of the prime-order group.

The classical **symbolic encoding** is a single equation, $\mathsf{verify}(\mathsf{sign}(m, sk), m, \mathsf{pk}(sk)) = \text{true}$, which rules all of the above out by construction. A **proof-oriented model** with minimal assumptions states only what the scheme guarantees: *consistency* (verification is deterministic), *no forgery* (for an honestly generated public key, signatures cannot be forged) and *correctness*.

**Example: WS-Security X.509 mutual authentication** (an old web-service protocol). The initiator, with $sk_i$ and a certificate, sends $\mathsf{sig}_{sk_i}(REQ, t)$, its certificate, a timestamp, $\{REQ\}_k$ and $\{k\}_{pk_r}$; the responder answers $\{RESP\}_{k_2}$, $\{k_2\}_{pk_i}$ and $\mathsf{sig}_{sk_r}(RESP, t)$. The goal is that both parties authenticate the request and the response and can **match** them: the signature must confirm the content and **bind** the response to the request. In the attack model, a victim initiator tries to talk to an honest peer $r$; because the signatures identify neither a unique message nor a unique public key, a key-substitution attack breaks the binding. Computational proofs, by contrast, often assume an honest key-setup phase and only let the adversary compromise private keys: **they give no guarantee for the key-pair setup**.

  caption="Figure 2. The attack on WS-Security X.509 mutual authentication: the attacker builds a key pair under which the initiator’s signature still verifies, so the responder answers the attacker’s request while the initiator accepts the response."

### Challenges

- Blockchains must reject malicious keys, otherwise inconsistencies follow.
- The NIST post-quantum signature competition: candidates are submitted for standardisation, and the binding properties of the hashing choices matter.
- Diffie–Hellman: groups are encoded as prime-order groups or curves, with different classes of behaviour; Cloudflare's standard libraries and the Signal protocol (libsignal, used by WhatsApp and Facebook Messenger) depend on them. Proofs of security often do not apply to the computational details (a limitation).

### Forward secrecy and post-compromise security

| Property | Attacker | Guarantee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (Perfect) forward secrecy | Gets all key pairs today | Cannot decrypt yesterday's messages |
| Post-compromise security (PCS) | Controls the network and compromises the device at some point | Future messages become secure again once the protocol **heals**, as soon as the attacker stops paying attention for a single exchange |

<SourceFigure
  id="cremers2026-pcs"
  caption="Figure 3. Forward secrecy protects messages sent before a compromise; post-compromise security protects messages after the protocol has healed."
/>

The **asymmetric ratchet** of Signal gives PCS: each request–response exchange (a ping-pong) carries a fresh Diffie–Hellman share from which the next message keys are derived. Because messaging is asynchronous, each user uploads a batch of **prekeys** to the server; a sender asks the server for an ephemeral key and falls back to a prekey if none is left.


## 2. Fully homomorphic encryption: foundations

**In short.** Homomorphic encryption computes on ciphertexts. Every operation adds noise; levelled schemes budget it, and bootstrapping refreshes it by decrypting homomorphically with an encrypted key. LWE is the noisy encoding that makes this secure.

### Definition and first examples

Homomorphic encryption lets a server evaluate a function on encrypted data: from $\mathsf{Enc}(x)$ it computes $\mathsf{Enc}(f(x))$ for an arbitrary $f$, without learning $x$.

**RSA is multiplicatively homomorphic.** With $N = p\,q$ for two large primes of the same size and $\mathsf{Enc}((e, N), m) = m^e \bmod N$:

$$
\mathsf{Enc}(m_0) \cdot \mathsf{Enc}(m_1) = m_0^e\, m_1^e = (m_0 m_1)^e = \mathsf{Enc}(m_0 m_1) \pmod N .
$$

**Goldwasser–Micali is XOR-homomorphic.** With $x$ a quadratic non-residue modulo $N$ and $\mathsf{Enc}(m) = r^2 x^m \bmod N$ for a bit $m$:

$$
c_0 c_1 = r_0^2 x^{m_0} \cdot r_1^2 x^{m_1} = (r_0 r_1)^2\, x^{m_0 + m_1} = \mathsf{Enc}(m_0 \oplus m_1),
$$

since $x^2$ is a square, so $x^{m_0 + m_1}$ only depends on $m_0 + m_1 \bmod 2$.

**Applications:** electronic voting; secure cloud computing (evaluation with a proof of correctness, without trusting the server); privacy-preserving machine learning (training or running a model without exposing the data, by encoding the functions homomorphically); privacy-preserving data analysis; scheme design.

### Secure computation techniques

| Technique | Protects |
| --- | --- |
| Multi-party computation | Several parties compute together on shared encrypted data |
| Fully homomorphic encryption | The **input**, before the computation |
| Secure enclaves, trusted execution environments | Computation inside protected hardware blocks |
| Differential privacy | The **output**, after the computation, by adding noise (a heat map of locations stays useful while individual positions are protected against inference) |

### The four algorithms

1. $\mathsf{Setup}(\lambda) \to (sk, evk)$: a secret key and an evaluation key.
2. $\mathsf{Encrypt}(sk, m) \to ct_m$.
3. $\mathsf{Eval}(evk, f, ct_{m_0}, \dots, ct_{m_{k-1}}) \to ct$: evaluates the circuit $f$.
4. $\mathsf{Decrypt}(sk, ct) \to m$.

**Correctness**, for fresh and for evaluated ciphertexts (those not produced by $\mathsf{Encrypt}$):

$$
\mathsf{Decrypt}(sk, \mathsf{Encrypt}(sk, m)) = m, \qquad
\mathsf{Decrypt}\big(sk,\ \mathsf{Eval}(evk, f, ct_0, \dots, ct_{k-1})\big) = f(m_0, \dots, m_{k-1}).
$$

Implementations may only guarantee $m^* \approx m$; the gap between the formal definition and the implementation is a possible attack surface.

### Noise, levels and bootstrapping

Ciphertexts are **noisy**, and every operation increases the noise; past a limit decryption fails.

- **Levelled HE:** the multiplicative depth of the circuit is fixed in advance and the parameters give a matching *noise budget*. It is fast (high-throughput SIMD operations) but limited to circuits whose depth is known beforehand.
- **Fully HE with bootstrapping:** unlimited depth. When the noise reaches the limit, **bootstrapping** turns the ciphertext into one with small noise, so the depth becomes dynamic and bootstrapping gates are placed only where needed.

**Bootstrapping, step by step.** Let $[m] = \mathsf{Encrypt}(sk, m)$, so $\mathsf{Decrypt}(sk, [m]) = m$.

1. Fix the noisy ciphertext $c = [m]$ and define the function $\mathsf{Decrypt}_{c}(\cdot) = \mathsf{Decrypt}(\cdot, c)$, with the key as its input and $c$ as a public constant.
2. Publish $[sk] = \mathsf{Encrypt}(sk, sk)$, the secret key encrypted under itself (a circular-security assumption).
3. Evaluate homomorphically: $\mathsf{Boot}(c) = \mathsf{Eval}(\mathsf{Decrypt}_{c}, [sk]) = [\mathsf{Decrypt}(sk, c)] = [m]$.

The result encrypts the same $m$, with the noise of one decryption circuit instead of the accumulated noise: the message stays hidden throughout.

### The FHE pipeline and LWE

$$
\text{message} \xrightarrow{\text{encode}} \text{plaintext} \xrightarrow{\text{encrypt}} \text{ciphertext (fresh)} \xrightarrow{\text{eval}} \text{ciphertext (evaluated)} \xrightarrow{\text{decrypt}} \text{plaintext} \xrightarrow{\text{decode}} \text{message}
$$

The noise must stay bounded for decryption to be correct, which is what **LWE** encryption, a noisy encoding scheme, controls. CKKS encodes complex numbers efficiently by rounding, at the cost of precision, managed with a scaling factor.

**LWE encryption.** With a secret $s \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n$ (for example binary), $n \in [600, 900]$ depending on the security target, a uniform $a \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n$ and a small Gaussian noise $e$:

$$
b = \langle a, s \rangle + e + u \pmod q, \qquad b - \langle a, s \rangle = u + e ,
$$

where $u$ encodes the message. Decryption recovers $u + e$, and rounding removes $e$ if it is small. Security: many samples $(a_i, b_i)$ are indistinguishable from uniform pairs in $\mathbb{Z}_q^{n+1}$.

Where the message sits in $b$: either in the **most significant bits**, $b = \langle a, s\rangle + e + \Delta m$ with $\Delta = \lfloor q/t \rfloor$ and the noise in the low bits, or in the least significant bits with the noise above it. Packing several values into the slots of one ciphertext (a vector of scalars) gives SIMD operations.

### Operations

- **Relinearisation.** A product of two ciphertexts has a term in $s^2$, i.e. three components. A key-switching key encrypting $s^2$ rewrites it with $k_0 + k_1 s \approx s^2$, back to two components.
- **External product** with a GSW-style ciphertext: $C = Z + m\,G$, where $Z$ is a matrix of RLWE encryptions of 0 and $G$ the gadget matrix; the gadget decomposition $G^{-1}$ keeps the noise small.
- **Multiplexer (CMux)** over an encrypted bit $b \in \{0, 1\}$ that nobody knows:
  $$\mathrm{mux}(b, m_1, m_0) = m_0 + b\,(m_1 - m_0).$$


## 3. Zero-knowledge proofs

**In short.** A proof system convinces a verifier that $x$ is in a language without revealing the witness. Schnorr is the basic example; Fiat–Shamir removes interaction; SNARKs such as Plonk combine a polynomial IOP with a polynomial commitment and reduce everything to checks on polynomials at random points.

### Languages and relations

- $L \in \mathsf{P}$ $\iff$ there is an algorithm $A$ with $A(x) = 1 \iff x \in L$, running in time $\mathrm{poly}(|x|)$: the language is decided.
- $L \in \mathsf{NP}$ $\iff$ there is a relation $R$ with $x \in L \iff \exists w : R(x, w) = 1$, checkable in time $\mathrm{poly}(|x| + |w|)$: the language is verified.
- Given $R$, define $L_R = \{x : \exists w,\ R(x, w) = 1\}$. The statement $x$ is public, the witness $w$ private.

### Proof systems

A two-party protocol between a prover $P(x, w)$ and a verifier $V(x)$, exchanging messages $m_1, c_1, \dots, m_T$; the verifier outputs accept or reject.

| Property | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Completeness** | $\forall (x, w) \in R$: $\Pr[\langle P(x, w), V(x)\rangle = 1] \approx 1$ |
| **Soundness** | $\forall \tilde P$, $\forall x \notin L_R$: $\Pr[\langle \tilde P(x), V(x)\rangle = 1] \le \kappa(|x|)$, negligible |
| **Knowledge soundness** | If $\tilde P$ convinces $V$, an extractor can get a witness from it, except with negligible probability: the prover *knows* a witness, not just that one exists |
| **Zero knowledge** | $\forall \tilde V$ $\exists$ a PPT simulator $S$: $\forall (x, w) \in R$, $S(x) \approx \mathrm{View}(\tilde V, x, w)$ (computationally, statistically or perfectly) |

The verifier must be convinced in a non-trivial way: the trivial proof for NP ($P$ sends $w$, $V$ checks $R(x, w)$) is complete and sound but not zero knowledge. Zero knowledge means a malicious verifier learns only that $x \in L_R$: its view, the sequence of messages, could have been produced by a simulator without $w$.

A trivial example of why soundness alone is weak: in a prime-order elliptic-curve group $(\mathbb{G}, +)$ with generator $G$ and $R = \{(P, k) : P = k \cdot G\}$, every $P \in \mathbb{G}$ has some $k$, so a verifier that only checks $P \in \mathbb{G}$ is sound ($L_R \setminus \mathbb{G} = \varnothing$) yet learns nothing about whether the prover knows $k$. That is what knowledge soundness captures.

### Fiat–Shamir

<SourceFigure
  id="feneuil2026-fiat-shamir"
  caption="Figure 5. Fiat–Shamir: each challenge is a hash of the message and of what the prover sent so far, so the prover alone produces a transcript that the verifier checks."
/>

A **public-coin** interactive protocol becomes non-interactive by replacing each random challenge with a hash of the transcript so far, $\rho_i \leftarrow H(x, m_1, \dots)$. Zero knowledge still holds: there is a PPT $S$ with $S(x) \approx \mathrm{View}(V, x, w)$, because the simulator controls the hash function and knows what the challenges must be.

### Schnorr's protocol

Group $(\mathbb{G}, +)$ of prime order $q$ where discrete log is hard; $R_{DLOG} = \{(K, k) : K = k \cdot G\} \subseteq \mathbb{G} \times \mathbb{Z}_q$.

1. The prover picks $w$ uniformly in $\mathbb{Z}_q$ and sends $W = w \cdot G$.
2. The verifier sends a random challenge $c$ drawn uniformly from $\mathbb{Z}_q$.
3. The prover sends $z = w + c\,k \bmod q$.
4. The verifier accepts iff $z \cdot G = W + c \cdot K$.

*Completeness:* $z \cdot G = w \cdot G + c\,k \cdot G = W + c\,K$. *Zero knowledge:* the randomness $w$ hides $k$; a malicious verifier sees $z$, which is uniform. *Soundness* rests on discrete log.

:::note[About the notes]
The notes write $K + c\,W = z \cdot G$ and $z = k + c\,w$, with the roles of $w$ and $k$ swapped. The standard form above multiplies the challenge by the secret $k$, so that the random $w$ masks it.
:::

### Reductions of knowledge

A probabilistic protocol can turn a claim $(x, w) \in R$ into a claim $(x', w') \in R'$ that implies it: Schnorr reduces $R_{DLOG}$ to the claim $(Z, z) \in R'_{DLOG}$ with $Z = W + c\,K$, and if $(Z, z) \in R'$ then $(K, k) \in R$. Chaining $R \to R' \to R'' \to \dots$, each step turns the statement being proven into a simpler problem that supports the next verification; transcripts and their distance are the tools.

### SNARKs from PIOPs and polynomial commitments

**PIOP + polynomial commitment ⇒ argument of knowledge (SNARK).**

- **Commitments** (non-interactive): *binding*, no one can find $M \ne M'$ with $\mathsf{Commit}(M) = \mathsf{Commit}(M')$; *hiding*, $\mathsf{Commit}(M) \approx \mathsf{Commit}(M')$ for all $M, M'$, thanks to randomness.
- **Polynomial commitments** (KZG): commit to $p$, then prove $p(u) = v$ at a chosen point. **Extractability**, stronger than binding: there is an extractor $E$ with $\Pr[\tilde P \text{ outputs } C \wedge E^{\tilde P} \not\to M] \le \kappa$; whoever commits knows the polynomial behind it.

### Plonk

| Tool | Statement |
| --- | --- |
| **Equality test** | Two committed polynomials $[f], [g]$ of degree $\le d$: check $f(x) = g(x)$ at a random point $x$ drawn uniformly from $\mathbb{F}$. By **Schwartz–Zippel**, if $f \ne g$ this passes with probability at most $d / |\mathbb{F}|$. Batching and linearisation keep the cost near $\deg$. |
| **Zero test** | $f$ vanishes on a set $H$ iff the vanishing polynomial $Z_H(X) = \prod_{a \in H}(X - a)$ divides $f$. With $H$ the $n$-th roots of unity, $Z_H(X) = X^n - 1$, which is fast to evaluate. |
| **Product check** | $\prod_{a \in H} f(a) = 1$ via an accumulator $t$ with $t(\omega x) = t(x)\, f(\omega x)$; rational version $t(\omega x)\,g(\omega x) = t(x)\,f(\omega x)$. |
| **Permutation check** | Two polynomials take the same values on $H$ up to a permutation; reduced to a product check, which lowers the complexity. |

**Circuit satisfiability** $R = \{(C, \vec x, y) : C(\vec x) = y\}$ with fan-in-2 gates (each constraint has two input edges in the directed graph of the circuit). The steps:

1. **Arithmetisation**: write the computation as a table (gates, degree, the trace of values).
2. **Gate constraints**: polynomials that hold at every row exactly when each gate computes correctly.
3. **Wire constraints**: a permutation check that copies of the same value agree, consistent with the previous computations.
4. Commit to $(W, \text{trace})$ as $[f], [g], [id]$ and check the constraints with the tests above.


## 4. Hash-based zero knowledge for post-quantum signatures

**In short.** Proving knowledge of the solution to a hard problem, with Fiat–Shamir, gives a signature. Using only hash-based commitments (GGM or Merkle trees) keeps it post-quantum; the hard problem (MQ, MinRank, lattices, codes) sets the statement and the size.

### From ZK proofs to signatures

- Completeness: $\Pr[\text{verify} \mid \text{honest prover}] = 1$; soundness: $\Pr[\text{verify} \mid \text{malicious prover}] \le \varepsilon$; zero knowledge: the verifier learns nothing about $w$.
- An **identification scheme** proves knowledge of a secret key; Fiat–Shamir turns it into a **signature**. The size of the statement drives performance, and the techniques vary with it.
- Soundness must be made negligible: enlarging the set of possible challenge values (costly) or parallel repetition.

### Hash-based commitments

Only hash functions are used, so the scheme stays post-quantum.

- **GGM tree:** seeds derived from a root seed; revealing all leaves except one ($i^*$) costs only the seeds on one path.

<SourceFigure

/>

<SourceFigure

  caption="Figure 7. A Merkle tree over the evaluations of a polynomial: opening one leaf needs only its authentication path."
/>

- **Merkle tree:** each leaf is hashed, so opening a value needs only its authentication path, which also ensures the committed polynomial has the right degree; each leaf is related to one evaluation.
- Side-channel attacks can probe or forge the randomness and interfere with the binding.
- Arithmetic over an extension field $\mathbb{K} \cong \mathbb{F}[x]/(x^r + 1)$, with a map $\psi : \mathbb{F}^t \to \mathbb{K}$.

### Building signatures from hard problems

The public key hides a trapdoor; the private key is the solution of an NP-hard problem, and the proof shows knowledge of it.

| Family | Statement | Constraint to prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rank-based** (MinRank) | $L \cdot R = \sum_i x_i M_i$ | Degree-2 polynomial constraints (MinRank schemes); a large proof statement |
| **Lattice-based** | $u = A x$ with $\|x\|_\infty \le \beta$ | Each coordinate is in $[-\beta, \beta]$: $R(x_i) = 0$ for $R(X) = (X + \beta)(X + \beta - 1)\cdots(X - \beta)$; binary decompositions use $\{0, 1\}$ |
| **Code-based** | $S \cdot Q = P \cdot F$ with $\deg Q = w$ | Two polynomials; a larger degree increases the signature size |

**Advanced signatures:** anonymous signatures, ring schemes over lists of users' keys, identity protection. Performance (proof size, running time) depends on the verification algorithm.


## 5. Homomorphic encryption in practice

**In short.** Rounding removes small noise, encrypted selection vectors give private lookups, and TFHE bootstraps by blind rotation: it rotates a test polynomial by an encrypted exponent through a chain of CMux gates.

### Operations and applications

- Rounding $[x] = \lfloor x + \tfrac{1}{2} \rfloor$ removes noise: $[m + e] = [m]$ if $|e| < \tfrac{1}{2}$.
- **Private lookup:** to read row $b$ of a public table without revealing which, send the encrypted selection vector $[0], [1], [0]$; the server returns $[0]\,a + [1]\,b + [0]\,c$, which decrypts to $b$.

### Bootstrapping in TFHE

The phase $b - \langle a, s \rangle$ lives on the torus $\mathbb{T}$; it is mapped to $\mathbb{Z}_{2N}$ (modulus switching), then decryption is done in the exponent of $X$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^N + 1)$, where $X^N = -1$ and $X^{2N} = 1$.

1. **Step 1:** $b - \langle a, s \rangle = \mathrm{encode}(m) + e$.
2. **Step 2 (decode):** round, $\mathrm{round}(\mathrm{decode}(\cdot))$, by reading a coefficient of $v(X) \cdot X^{\,b - \langle a, s\rangle}$ for a test polynomial $v$.
3. **Rotation:** multiplying by $X^i$ rotates the coefficients, $[m(X)]\,X^i = (a(X) X^i,\ b(X) X^i)$.
4. **Blind rotation:** with the bits $p_i$ of the exponent encrypted ($p = \sum_i 2^i p_i$), a chain of CMux gates computes $[m(X)]\,X^{p}$: at step $i$, choose between $[m(X)]$ and $[m(X)]\,X^{2^i}$ depending on $[p_i]$ ($p_i = 0 \Rightarrow m(X)$, $p_i = 1 \Rightarrow m(X)\,X^{2^i}$).
5. **Sign function:** in $\mathbb{Z}_4 = \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$, $\{0, 1\}$ maps to $+$ and $\{2, 3\}$ to $-$; because $X^N = -1$, a constant test polynomial returns $+1$ or $-1$.

A **trivial ciphertext** has no noise. Blind rotation changes the secret key under which the value is encrypted without ever decrypting. **HE processors** chain these steps to evaluate $f(m)$ from $X^{-2N(m+e)}$-style inputs.


## 6. Electronic voting

**In short.** A voting protocol must record ballots as cast, keep votes private and stay verifiable. Homomorphic tallying sums encrypted ballots; re-randomisation and proofs stop ballot copying.

- **Recorded-as-cast:** the ballot box is a public bulletin board (for example a blockchain). **Cast-or-audit** (a Benaloh challenge): the voter can either cast the ballot or open it to check it was built correctly. A ballot looks like $b = (\{X\}^{r_X}_{pk}, \{A\}^{r_A}_{pk}, \{B\}^{r_B}_{pk}, \mathrm{zkp}(B = X + A \bmod n))$.
- **Vote privacy:** only the result may leak; the two scenarios $\text{Alice}(0) \mid \text{Bob}(1)$ and $\text{Alice}(1) \mid \text{Bob}(0)$ must be indistinguishable.
- **Homomorphic tally:** $\mathsf{enc}(x, pk) \cdot \mathsf{enc}(y, pk) = \mathsf{enc}(x + y, pk)$, so ballots are summed without being opened.
- **Ballot copying:** encryption is not enough, since an attacker can copy Alice's ballot and learn how she voted from the result. Re-randomisation makes copies unrecognisable, $\mathsf{enc}(x, pk, r) \cdot \mathsf{enc}(0, pk, r') = \mathsf{enc}(x, pk, r + r')$, and proofs must bind ballots to voters.
- **Verifiability attacks:** include the order of the proofs to prevent tampering.
- **Hybrid security:** combine a classical and a post-quantum assumption, so the scheme stays secure if one of the two holds.

## 8. The privacy–utility trade-off

**In short.** Security protects systems; privacy protects people. Anonymity, unlinkability, minimisation and differential privacy are the requirements, and $k$-anonymity, $\ell$-diversity and $t$-closeness are successive answers for data sets.

### Security versus privacy

**Security** protects systems and information from unauthorised access and modification (policy enforcement). Its properties:

| Property | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Confidentiality | Only authorised access |
| Integrity | No unauthorised modification |
| Availability | Service and transmission available |
| Authenticity | Data comes from a trusted source (signature schemes) |
| Accountability | Actions can be traced to a security goal |
| Non-repudiation | Authorship of an action cannot be denied |

**Privacy** is personal-data protection: identity (location and so on) and activity leakage.

### Privacy requirements

- **Anonymity:** access a resource or service without leaking identity, hidden in an *anonymity set*. Levels: complete anonymity (no de-anonymisation), pseudo-anonymity (de-anonymisation possible when necessary, for instance for a crime) and pseudonymity (a virtual identity). Anonymity allows authentication without leaking who, hiding the user in a specified set.
- **Indistinguishability:** one cannot be told from another.
- **Unlinkability:** actions cannot be linked to each other or to a property of other entities of the same class.
- **Data minimisation:** the provider knows the minimum about its customers.
- **Unobservability:** retrieved data reveals nothing more about identity.
- **Differential privacy:** adding or removing one person changes the output distribution by at most a bounded amount; noise is added so that no statistical difference reveals whether a data point was included.

### Anonymising data sets

A micro-data set has **identifiers**, **key attributes** (quasi-identifiers) and **sensitive attributes**.

| Technique | Requirement | Weakness addressed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $k$-anonymity | Each record is indistinguishable from at least $k - 1$ others on the quasi-identifiers | Re-identification by linking |
| $\ell$-diversity | Each equivalence class has at least $\ell$ well-represented sensitive values | Attribute-disclosure attacks; but it does not ensure the values are semantically dissimilar |
| $t$-closeness | The distribution of sensitive values in each class is within distance $t$ of the overall distribution | Skewness and similarity attacks |


## 9. Information-flow analysis

**In short.** Non-interference says secrets must not influence public outputs; a type system over a security lattice checks it, and timing is a channel too.

- **Program properties:** an attacker who only waits and measures can compute the execution time of an algorithm, so timing is an information channel.
- **Security lattices** order security levels: linear (NATO-style levels) or security zones (connected by edges). They matter for post-quantum cryptographic code as for any other.
- **Type-based approach** to **non-interference**: high-security inputs must not influence low-security outputs. Two states are **$L$-equivalent** if they agree on every variable of level $L$ or lower; a program is non-interferent if running it from $L$-equivalent states always gives $L$-equivalent results, whatever the high (secret) values.


## 10. Quantitative information flow

**In short.** When some leakage is unavoidable, measure it: leakage is the drop in uncertainty about the secret, $H(S) - H(S \mid O)$.

- **Motivation:** confidentiality against observations correlated with a secret. Information flow is leakage plus interference.
- **Intrinsic leakage:** a password checker that answers "wrong" lets an adversary learn something (length, prefix). A **side-channel attack** measures the execution time of a checker that stops at the first wrong character, and recovers the longest correct prefix.
- **DC-nets:** a set of nodes in which one source broadcasts one bit of information anonymously.
- **Crowds** (anonymous communication): **probable innocence** holds when the probability that the detected node is the real sender is at most $\tfrac{1}{2}$.
- **Channels:** a probabilistic system is a noisy channel from secrets to observations (one output can come from different inputs). **Strong anonymity:** the observation does not change the prior, e.g. $\Pr(0) = \Pr(1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$ before and after (Bayes' theorem).
- **Entropy** $H(S)$ measures the uncertainty about the secret. The **leakage** is the reduction of uncertainty after the observation:
  $$I(S; O) = H(S) - H(S \mid O).$$

**Example: guessing with yes/no questions.** To find one of seven secrets $\{a, \dots, g\}$, ask "is it in $\{a, b\}$?", then "$\{a\}$?" or "$\{c, d\}$?", and so on down a binary tree. Each answer is one bit of observation; the expected number of questions is close to $H(S)$, and each question leaks the information it removes.
