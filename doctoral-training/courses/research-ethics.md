# Research ethics and scientific integrity

A six-hour, French-language seminar on research ethics, scientific integrity and professional conduct (*Éthique de la recherche, intégrité scientifique et déontologie*). Programme on [ADUM](https://adum.fr/phd/formation/catalogue.pl?mod=3783710&parent=moduleaccept).

| --- | --- |
| Date | 25 November 2026, 09:00–12:00 and 14:00–17:00 |
| Duration | 6 hours |
| Language | French |
| Venue | Cnam, Amphi Fourastié, Paris |
| Status | Attended; mandatory |
| Assessment | Attendance (50%) and an ethics case study applied to the doctoral project (50%) |

## What the course covers

- Ethics, morality, scientific integrity and professional conduct: how they differ, and the founding principles applied to research.
- Frameworks: the Nuremberg Code, the Declaration of Helsinki, the French national charter and the European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity; the role of ethics review bodies.
- Research involving people: the Jardé law (RIPH), data protection, participant information, informed consent and individual rights.
- Responsible management, hosting, confidentiality and security of research data.
- Misconduct (fabrication, falsification, plagiarism, publication fraud), conflicts of interest, probity and the researcher's responsibilities.
- Applying ethical reasoning to a case taken from the doctoral project.

## Notes — lesson 1: research ethics and scientific integrity (level 1)

### 1. Introduction

- **Topic.** What ethics means in computer science research.
- **Scope.** Scientific integrity: what a researcher must do, and how it is done in practice (*devenir / savoir en pratique chercheur*).
- **Objectives of the course.**
  1. Understand what is at stake: publication policy, social consequences.
  2. Tell the key notions apart: ethics, integrity, professional conduct.
  3. Understand the ethics bodies (*instances d'éthique*) and why they work differently from one sector to another.

### 2. Ethics: a formal definition

Ethics is a **branch of philosophy**. It asks for a **rational justification**, the reason why something is held to be true, and it bears on the **decisions taken about research**.

#### 2.1 Three words that are often confused

| Term | Applies to | In practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Morality** (*morale*) | Global rules of a society | Collective ↔ individual, settled by discussion |
| **Ethics** (*éthique*) | Personal behaviour, oneself | What I decide to do, and why |
| **Professional conduct** (*déontologie*) | Decisions inside a profession, a team, a tool (an AI tool, for example) | Professional rules that regulate collaborations and development work; decided for the majority, they also protect you (compare the labour code) |

An example from software work: the many attempts (*tentatives*) at writing code are not what counts. What counts is the algorithm or solution that the team has thought through and agreed on.

**Morality and responsibility.** Responsibility lies with whoever decides or acts, engineers included. It means weighing the important risks before acting.

**Three ways of reasoning about a decision.**

| Approach | Question it asks | Notes from the session |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Duty (deontology) | Which rule applies? | Rules decided for the majority; codes of conduct, labour code |
| Virtue ethics | What would an honest, prudent, responsible person do? | Honesty, prudence, responsibility |
| Consequences | What will the action cause? | Consequences are assessed **before** acting: evaluate the technology against its effect on the population, with human dignity and a view of the world in mind |

**Ethics of responsibility.** Two extensions:

- the **precautionary principle**;
- **intergenerational** responsibility, towards those who will live with the consequences.

**The ethics triangle.** Three poles to balance in a decision: **responsibility**, **conviction** and **dignity**.

#### 2.2 Discourse ethics

- Ethics of communication: how we speak together.
- The basis for **collective decisions**, by grouping ideas.
- **Social justification**: the mood and response of the people involved (research carried out freely and with motivation). It sits at the political level, as a point of balance, and requires telling the different layers (individual, team, society) apart.

### 3. Values and ethical stance

- **Cultural schemas** in digital media, such as violence or racism: how to adjust behaviour, and how to limit it by a deliberate act.
- **Positive judgement** (a positive value), with criteria such as people already known to the researchers or the presence of **sensitive data**. Politics and society weigh on the group of researchers.
- **Awareness of risks.** Do not presume that everyone in the team accepts; think within the specific professional context; anticipate and plan solutions that minimise the problems of a system (nuclear power was the example).
- **Technology production and moral law.** Producing technology (AI, data centres) has to be discussed against moral rules. Work performance is built into collective arrangements.
  - Generalising a problem turns it into a **question of conscience**: taking risks or not.
  - An **ethics community** (care for people, review and analysis) handles the complexity, which leads to **laws and regulation** being applied.
- **Ethics by design.** Ethics is bounded by the design of the application and by digital reflection, done by the designer or engineer through a **collective decision**. It also covers elements such as the images on a poster.

### 4. Integrity

Integrity covers:

- **governance rules**;
- how **code and computations** are managed, which follows from the **research methodology**;
- the **reliability of data**;
- the way **co-authors** are added: adding someone who did not contribute is put on the same footing as **data fabrication**;
- the **protection of people's rights**, together with a **reproducible method**;
- how knowledge is **communicated**: apply results, but place them in a frame. **Model and prove** (evaluate the theory), so that the work can directly support, produce and finally propose something.

**Political dimensions of science, for society.**

- The topics chosen, for example in AI (models that are forbidden), affect **trust in research**, and this has to be settled within a thesis.
- Instruments that affect the public (professional organisations) depend on the type of engagement, including the language used.

### 5. Fundamental principles of research ethics

1. **Maximise the benefit**, for the person and for society.
2. **Respect rights and dignity.**
3. **Minimise risks and dangers.**
4. **Responsibilities and obligations.**
5. **Independence**, in particular when the research intervenes on human beings.

Further points from the end of the session:

- Research must be able to **evolve**, against the risks and the precautions they call for.
- Research across domains involving people falls under a **committee for the protection of persons** (CPP, *comité de protection des personnes*), with an obligation to consult it and to be controlled by it. The committee accepts or refuses the protocol.
- **Rule out the dead ends** by writing the protocol in advance (how the work will be done and produced): the obligations that apply, and a methodology that is clear and well thought out. This is the **guarantee of quality**.

### Preparing the case study

The assessment asks for an ethics case study on the doctoral project. Questions to work from, for certified reconfiguration of TSN:

- **Reliability of data.** Are benchmark stream sets, testbed measurements and simulation seeds published so that the bounds can be reproduced?
- **Precaution.** What happens when an admission decision is wrong in a safety-critical network? Which assumptions does the certificate rest on, and are they stated?
- **Authorship.** Who contributed to each result, and how is the use of AI tools for code and writing declared?
- **Responsibility.** Who is responsible for a reconfiguration that the controller accepted but that fails in the field?
